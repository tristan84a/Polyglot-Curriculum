POLYGLOT CURRICULUM CHARTER

Version: 0.8
Status: Current
Encoding: UTF-8

1. PURPOSE

The Polyglot Curriculum Charter defines durable principles for designing
language curricula. It deliberately does not attempt to automate every
language-specific decision.

The governing hierarchy is:

Charter → Language Syllabus → Curriculum Manifest → Card CSV

The Charter supplies principles. A language syllabus supplies a useful
path forward. The manifest records progress and ID allocation. The CSV
contains the actual authored cards. Learner scheduling state belongs to
the app, not to curriculum design.

A syllabus is a map, not a transcript of every step taken while cards are
generated. A sound card sequence may take a prerequisite-safe detour,
introduce a useful later structure early, or postpone part of an earlier
unit without requiring a new syllabus version. Amend the map when the
preferred path or destination changes, not merely because generation took
a local detour.

2. CENTRAL MODEL

The curriculum is static; the scheduler is adaptive.

Each language has an independent canonical curriculum. New material is
introduced according to prerequisite knowledge, linguistic structure,
usefulness, natural usage, and instructional clarity. Learner performance
changes scheduling, not curriculum order.

The completed deck should read as a coherent curriculum even though it is
built incrementally. Card generation is therefore both implementation and
discovery: early cards may reveal that a language needs a somewhat
different route than was anticipated.

3. SCOPE

Polyglot moves toward a hypothetical educated-adult complete set rather
than a finite certification endpoint.

Vocabulary includes ordinary vocabulary, important senses, collocations,
idiom, phraseology, register, semantic distinctions, institutional and
educated discourse, and a long tail comparable to what an educated adult
speaker might reasonably know or encounter. It does not mean every
attested word or specialist term.

Grammar includes enough morphology, syntax, discourse, pragmatics and
orthography that ordinary modern use is not left systematically
unexplained. Literacy is curriculum content where the language requires
it.

As a curriculum matures, difficulty need not rise monotonically. Grammar
eventually ceases to be the main organizing axis; breadth, collocation,
register, semantic precision and natural usage increasingly dominate.
Later IDs represent farther travel through the language, not a requirement
for increasingly obscure or syntactically difficult cards.

4. DOCUMENTATION-LIMITED LANGUAGES

Full educated-adult depth is the default.

A syllabus may explicitly designate a language documentation-limited only
when reliable resources available to Polyglot are insufficient to support
that objective confidently. This describes a limitation of documentation
and generation capability, never a limitation of the language.

Proceed as deeply and broadly as reliable evidence permits. The frontier
may be uneven. Stop or leave an area unresolved rather than fabricate
vocabulary, constructions, idiom or supposedly natural usage. Improved
resources may later move the frontier outward.

5. LANGUAGE INDEPENDENCE AND LEARNING BY EXAMPLE

Every language has its own path. Do not distort one language to imitate
another.

At the same time, successful Polyglot curricula are important design
evidence. New languages should learn from existing examples rather than
re-inventing solutions merely for originality. French, German and Japanese
now provide deliberately reworked examples of substantially different
openings; Hungarian, Turkish, Polish and later languages provide further
evidence as they mature.

Examples are anchors, not templates. Copy pacing, card architecture,
controlled-example techniques, lexical choices or structural sequences
closely when they fit. Diverge whenever the target language gives a real
linguistic or pedagogical reason.

The practical default is:

understand the language → inspect useful Polyglot examples → build a small
seed → discuss what the seed reveals → establish a path forward → continue
in progressively larger batches.

Some languages will reveal their path quickly. Others will require more
discussion or experimentation. The documents need not predict every
special case in advance.

6. SMALL SEED AND EARLY PRODUCTIVE LANGUAGE

Begin with a small reusable lexical seed and move into productive language
as soon as the target language permits.

A useful default seed to test is:

house; man; woman; child; big; small; good; bad.

It is not mandatory. Japanese, for example, demonstrated that a small set
of nouns, adjectives and concrete verbs can be a better opening because it
supports the structures Japanese naturally wants to introduce.

The bootstrap exists to make productive structure possible, not to build a
miniature dictionary. Long runs of isolated nouns, adjectives, adverbs or
infinitives before useful phrases and sentences are normally a pacing
failure.

Vocabulary then grows just in time alongside grammar. New words should
create opportunities for structure, and new structure should create demand
for useful words.

7. PREREQUISITES, DETOURS, AND ORDER

Prerequisites matter; arbitrary seriality does not.

A new pattern should be clear enough that another novelty does not obscure
it, but independent concepts need not be exhaustively established one at a
time. Numbered syllabus units primarily indicate useful introduction order,
dependencies and editorial organization.

Canonical row order records a coherent introduction path. It does not imply
that adjacent cards must be presented adjacently to the learner. Polyglot
ultimately shuffles available cards, so every card should stand on its own
without depending on the immediately preceding card.

A local deviation from syllabus order is acceptable when it produces
natural, clear language and does not create an unresolved prerequisite
problem. Repeated deviations may reveal that the syllabus itself should
change; a single sensible detour normally does not.

8. INTRODUCTION, ESTABLISHMENT, AND REUSE

INTRODUCED means a concept has begun to be demonstrated.

ESTABLISHED means the canonical curriculum has demonstrated its basic
operation sufficiently that later cards may assume it.

REUSABLE means established material may appear naturally without being the
principal teaching objective.

Introduction is not establishment. After a controlled introduction, later
cards should deliberately reuse earlier questions, negation, number, case,
tense, agreement, particles, word order and vocabulary while teaching new
material. Double-duty cards are normally preferable to large isolated
practice blocks.

The scheduler supplies repetition of individual cards. The curriculum
supplies varied examples and expanding contexts. Exact duplicate cards
therefore have no curricular purpose and should not occur.

9. RULE BEFORE EXCEPTION AND LEARNING THROUGH EXAMPLES

Teach productive/default patterns clearly before competing patterns or
exceptions when the language permits. High-frequency irregular material
may appear early when ordinary usage requires it.

Grammar is normally taught through controlled target-language examples,
not cards asking for grammatical terminology. Early examples may be more
controlled; later examples should become increasingly natural and
integrated.

Not every form needs a standalone introductory card before it appears.
Once the learner has enough structure to infer a new form safely,
comprehensible discovery is legitimate curriculum design.

10. PARADIGMS AND SYSTEMATIC COVERAGE

Systematic paradigms are useful when their cells teach genuine contrasts.
They are wasteful when they merely substitute new lexical stems into an
already-known pattern.

A practical test is:

If filling every cell teaches a new contrast, fill the table. If it merely
repeats an established table with another stem, sample and reuse instead.

This principle applies to conjugation, declension, agreement, classifiers,
particles and other structured inventories. Do not swing to either extreme:
neither recite every possible paradigm mechanically nor avoid systematic
coverage when the distinctions themselves are important.

11. VOCABULARY, PHRASEOLOGY, AND NATURAL USAGE

Early vocabulary should be disproportionately useful, concrete, frequent,
structurally productive or pedagogically valuable. Easily confused words
should normally be spaced unless contrast is the lesson.

As the grammatical scaffold matures, lexical freedom should increase.
Common words should develop longitudinally through important senses,
argument structures, collocations, idioms and register. Translation
equivalents are pedagogical mappings, not claims of identical semantic
range.

Mature lexical domains develop in parallel. Do not exhaust science before
arts, register before phraseology, or one arbitrary domain before opening
another. Difficulty may plateau while breadth continues for many thousands
of cards.

12. AUTHENTIC LANGUAGE, ORTHOGRAPHY, AND INPUT

Targets should be natural contemporary language for the intended meaning
and register. Do not preserve explicit subjects, pronouns or other English
structure merely because the English prompt contains them.

Use authentic conventional orthography from first exposure. Writing
systems may require language-specific pacing, but unfamiliar script alone
is not a reason to replace normal target-language spelling with
transliteration.

InputHint is practical input-method metadata, not an alternative answer.
Its detailed use belongs to the language syllabus or current experiment.

13. SYLLABI

A language syllabus should be useful enough to guide future generation
without pretending to foresee every card.

It should identify major structural dependencies, important language-
specific issues, broad development into mature usage, and any decisions
that future generators would otherwise have to rediscover.

Do not add complexity merely to encode every conceivable exception. When a
new language presents an uncertain design choice, discussion and a small
prototype are often better than another universal rule.

Completion standards may be used where they clarify what establishment
requires, but they are not card quotas and need not turn the syllabus into
a checklist of sealed blocks.

14. CARD DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Each card should have one intended Target under exact matching. Improve an
ambiguous English prompt rather than hiding ambiguity behind multiple
answers.

Lexical cards normally use the language's ordinary citation form. Grammar
should normally be embodied in useful words, phrases and sentences.

Cards should stand independently because scheduling may separate them.
Reinforcement should normally vary context rather than duplicate a card.

PartOfSpeech, InputHint and other schema conventions are implementation
details governed by the Card Generation Protocol.

15. DEVELOPMENT, STABLE IDS, AND LEARNER STATE

Before a deck has entered real learner use, pedagogical cleanup may justify
reordering or renumbering draft cards. Development cleanliness outranks
preserving accidental prototype history.

Once a card has entered learner state, its ID is a stable identity. Do not
reuse that ID for materially different curriculum content merely to tidy
the sequence. Ordinary corrections may preserve the ID so learner state
remains attached to the same card.

Git history is the archive for superseded development material. Obsolete
trial files need not remain beside canonical card files once their useful
lessons have been incorporated.

16. REVIEW AND HUMAN JUDGMENT

Polyglot is intentionally collaborative.

When a new language, script, grammatical system or generation decision
raises a meaningful fork, inspect examples, build a small test sequence,
and discuss the choice rather than inventing a universal rule prematurely.

The objective is not maximum automation. It is a coherent curriculum
produced efficiently with enough human judgment to notice when the target
language is telling us that the current plan should change.

17. AMENDMENTS

The Charter is versioned. Amend it when system-wide curriculum principles
change, not when routine generation advances a language.

Version 0.8 simplifies the governing model around principles, discussion
and learning by example. It formalizes the syllabus-as-roadmap principle,
prototype-led discovery, shuffle-independent cards, duplicate avoidance,
paradigm calibration, development-versus-live ID handling, and the
long-tail principle that breadth may continue after difficulty plateaus.
It replaces attempts to anticipate language-specific special cases with a
default of small experiments and explicit discussion where needed.

18. GOVERNING PRINCIPLE

Use simple durable rules where they genuinely generalize. Use successful
examples as design evidence. Let the target language determine necessary
divergence. Discuss meaningful uncertainty instead of burying it under
procedural complexity.
