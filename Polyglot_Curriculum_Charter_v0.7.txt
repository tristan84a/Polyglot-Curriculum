POLYGLOT CURRICULUM CHARTER

Version: 0.7 Status: Current Encoding: UTF-8

1. PURPOSE

The Polyglot Curriculum Charter governs the design of all language
curricula in Polyglot.

It defines the principles by which language-specific syllabi are
created, amended, reconciled with existing cards, and extended toward
mature competence. It does not prescribe one universal grammar sequence,
one fixed vocabulary list, or one fixed number of cards.

The Charter governs the syllabi. Each language syllabus governs the
canonical curriculum for that language. The curriculum manifest, where
used, records progress through those syllabi. The card CSV contains the
actual cards and scheduling state.

The intended hierarchy is:

Charter → Language Syllabi → Curriculum Manifest → Card CSV

Curriculum specification and curriculum progress are separate. Routine
card generation changes progress, not the Charter or syllabus.

2. CENTRAL MODEL

The curriculum is static; the scheduler is adaptive.

Each language has an independent canonical curriculum whose pedagogical
order is determined by prerequisite knowledge, linguistic structure,
usefulness, and instructional clarity. Learner performance may alter how
frequently established cards recur, but it does not alter the canonical
order in which new material is introduced.

The curriculum should be designed as though the entire language course
had already been constructed in advance, even when cards are actually
generated in batches.

This is the shrink-wrapped curriculum principle: a future observer
should be able to inspect the completed deck and see a coherent
pedagogical sequence that does not depend on knowing the learner’s
historical weights, mistakes, or scheduling history.

3. SCOPE OF A LANGUAGE COURSE

Polyglot moves toward a hypothetical complete set rather than a finite
certification endpoint.

For vocabulary, “complete” means approximately the vocabulary, idiom,
collocation, register, and semantic distinctions a typical educated
adult speaker might reasonably know and encounter. It does not mean
every attested word, every technical term, every dialect word, or every
historical form.

For grammar, “complete” means enough morphology, syntax, discourse
structure, pragmatics, orthography, and productive patterning that
ordinary modern use is not left systematically unexplained.

For languages requiring substantial script or literacy instruction,
literacy is part of the curriculum rather than an external prerequisite.

A language may therefore have an indefinitely expandable long tail.
Completeness describes direction and scope, not an absolute final card
count.

3A. DOCUMENTATION-LIMITED LANGUAGES
The scope described above is the default for every Polyglot language. A language must not be treated as having a reduced or finite curriculum merely because its later material is advanced, specialized, uncommon, difficult to generate, or less familiar to the curriculum author.
Exceptionally, a language syllabus may explicitly designate the language as documentation-limited when the reliable linguistic resources available to Polyglot are insufficient to support the normal educated-adult long-tail objective with acceptable confidence.
Documentation-limited status describes a limitation of the available documentation and generation capability, not a limitation of the language itself. It must not be interpreted as implying that the language lacks expressive capacity, grammatical sophistication, specialized vocabulary, literary potential, or the ability to support advanced modern use.
A language is documentation-limited only when its current approved syllabus explicitly says so. In the absence of that designation, the normal Polyglot completeness objective applies.
For a documentation-limited language, the curriculum should proceed as deeply and broadly as reliable knowledge permits. It may be extensive and may include advanced grammar, vocabulary, idiom, register, literature, professional language, or other sophisticated material wherever these can be supported reliably. “Partial” therefore does not mean “beginner” or prescribe a proficiency ceiling.
When reliable support becomes insufficient, the curriculum should stop or leave the relevant area unresolved rather than fabricate vocabulary, constructions, idiom, semantic distinctions, or supposedly natural usage merely to extend coverage or reach a card-count target.
The documentation frontier may be uneven. A language may support very deep treatment of some grammatical, lexical, cultural, or discourse domains while providing much thinner reliable material in others. The syllabus should reflect the available knowledge rather than impose artificial uniformity.
Documentation-limited status is revisable. Improved dictionaries, grammars, corpora, community-produced resources, terminology, scholarship, or other reliable evidence may move the curriculum frontier outward without invalidating earlier material.

4. LANGUAGE INDEPENDENCE

Every language has its own canonical syllabus.

Languages may be interleaved mechanically in the unified Polyglot deck,
but their pedagogical sequences are independent. Progress in French does
not determine what German should teach next; progress in German does not
determine what Japanese should teach next.

Cross-language similarities may inform curriculum design, but one
language must not be distorted to fit the grammatical categories or
teaching sequence of another.

Geographic, historical, typological, or learner-useful relationships
between languages may influence the broader branching concept of
Polyglot, but each language’s internal prerequisite structure remains
authoritative.

5. STANDARDIZATION AND LANGUAGE-SPECIFIC DIVERGENCE

Structural consistency across language syllabi is the default where it
is pedagogically neutral.

No element should be changed merely for the sake of making a language
course look different. Shared terminology, document organization, unit
fields, broad lexical stages, and other reusable structures should
remain consistent when they fit naturally.

However, consistency is subordinate to the language itself.

If even a modest change in sequence, unit structure, category, literacy
treatment, or instructional strategy is pedagogically preferable for a
particular language, the syllabus should make that change without
reluctance.

The standard is therefore:

Preserve common structure unless there is a real reason to diverge; when
there is a real reason, diverge as much as necessary and no more.

The Charter prescribes how a curriculum is constructed. It does not
prescribe which grammatical categories every language must contain.

6. PREREQUISITE-DRIVEN ORDERING

Every new card or unit should have an intelligible prerequisite
relationship to what precedes it.

A new card may introduce a new lexical item, grammatical pattern,
orthographic element, idiom, or controlled combination of these. Except
for the intended new material, the card should normally rely on
previously established knowledge.

Earlier material becomes reusable once sufficiently established by the
canonical curriculum.

The preferred progression is:

Small reusable lexical seed → immediate productive structure → just-in-time
lexical expansion alongside rapidly developing grammar → progressively
enrich grammar and vocabulary together → broaden vocabulary and natural
usage → develop the educated-adult long tail.

The bootstrap is deliberately small. Its purpose is to make the first
productive structures possible, not to accumulate a miniature dictionary
before grammar begins.

A highly reusable default seed for many languages is:

house; man; woman; child; big; small; good; bad.

This eight-item set is an editorial model, not an immutable universal
requirement. Translate or adapt it naturally, omit an item that is
structurally awkward or culturally inappropriate, and substitute another
item when the target language needs a better anchor. Where the language
permits it, however, reusing this seed across curricula is preferable to
inventing a different opening merely for originality.

Existing Polyglot card sequences may be copied closely when their pacing
and structures fit the target language. In particular, the early French
sequence is an approved pacing model: a few nouns and adjectives are
followed almost immediately by productive phrases and sentences, with new
vocabulary added in small increments as new structure becomes available.
Cross-language originality has no pedagogical value by itself.

This is a model, not a mandatory sequence of identical stages. Languages
may require case systems, classifiers, honorifics, evidentiality,
agreement systems, literacy strands, or other structures at different
points. Authentic orthography still applies from first exposure; writing
systems develop through the same real words and sentences rather than
through a detached transliteration bootstrap.

7. RULE BEFORE EXCEPTION

Teach one coherent pattern at a time.

Where a productive rule exists, establish the rule before introducing
exceptions that would obscure it. Where several competing constructions
exist, begin with the construction that best exposes the underlying
system or provides the most useful foundation.

Exceptions should not be artificially delayed when they are
indispensable to ordinary language, but they should be introduced in a
way that preserves the learner’s ability to see the productive pattern.

A high-frequency irregular form may therefore appear early when useful,
while still receiving explicit treatment as an exception or separate
pattern.

8. GRAMMAR THROUGH CONTROLLED EXAMPLES

Polyglot primarily teaches grammar through language, not through
questions about grammatical terminology.

A grammar concept should normally be established by a deliberately
ordered series of words, phrases, and sentences that makes the pattern
inferable and usable.

Terminology may be used in syllabi and implementation notes because it
helps design the curriculum. It need not become card content unless
knowing the term itself is useful.

The ordering of examples is part of the explanation.

A well-designed sequence should progressively remove ambiguity about how
the language works without requiring a prose grammar lesson before every
structure.

9. CONTINUOUS VOCABULARY ACQUISITION

Vocabulary acquisition is continuous throughout the curriculum.

At the beginning of a language, continuous vocabulary growth does not mean
front-loading a large vocabulary inventory. Begin with a small reusable
seed and introduce additional vocabulary just in time for structures that
can immediately use it. A succession of dozens of standalone nouns,
adjectives, adverbs, or infinitives before productive language begins is
normally a pacing failure, even when every individual word is common.

Grammar units define structural progression; they do not restrict
vocabulary to previously established lexical anchors. New vocabulary
should be introduced at a rate that permits both grammatical
comprehension and steady lexical growth.

The curriculum should avoid both extremes:

-   New grammar should not be obscured by a flood of unfamiliar
    vocabulary.
-   Vocabulary should not stagnate while a long grammatical sequence is
    taught with the same tiny set of words.
-   A nominal "bootstrap" should not delay useful phrases and sentences
    merely to build lexical variety in advance.

As the grammatical scaffold matures, lexical freedom should increase.

Later broad-vocabulary stages indicate that lexical breadth has become
the principal organizing purpose. They do not imply that vocabulary
acquisition begins there.

As the curriculum matures, numbered units increasingly define first
introduction, prerequisite relationships, and editorial organization rather
than giant blocks that must be mastered serially. Once their prerequisites
are available, mature domains such as register, phraseology, broad lexical
fields, educated-adult vocabulary, regional usage, and the long tail should
normally develop as longitudinal strands in parallel.

The numbered order remains meaningful: later strands should generally be
introduced in a sensible prerequisite-aware sequence. But introduction of
one mature strand does not require exhaustive completion before another
begins. A learner need not master register switching before phraseology
starts, nor exhaust scientific vocabulary before cultural, institutional,
or other lexical domains begin. Introduce mature strands when ready, then
revisit and deepen them repeatedly across the long tail.

10. LEXICAL SELECTION AND SEQUENCING

Early vocabulary should be disproportionately useful, concrete,
frequent, structurally productive, or pedagogically valuable.

Vocabulary selection should increasingly broaden toward ordinary adult
life, abstract concepts, educated discourse, institutional language,
culture, and the lower-frequency long tail.

Words that are easily confused should generally be spaced rather than
introduced as a dense cluster unless deliberate contrast is the teaching
objective.

Abstract verbs and nouns should not be introduced in large arbitrary
runs. When possible, lexical items should later recur in contexts that
reveal argument structure, collocation, morphology, register, or
semantic distinctions.

Frequency is important but not absolute. A somewhat less frequent word
may precede a more frequent one when it is a better lexical anchor or
prerequisite.

11. LEXICAL USAGE, POLYSEMY, AND PHRASEOLOGY

Knowing a lexical item includes more than knowing a basic translation.
The curriculum should progressively establish the important senses,
constructions, complements, collocations, idiomatic uses, semantic
boundaries, and register constraints associated with common words.

Lexical competence is continuous curriculum content alongside vocabulary
acquisition and grammar. It should develop throughout the course rather
than being postponed to a finite late-stage collection of idioms.

High-frequency polysemous words should develop longitudinally. Their core
meaning and morphology may be established early; additional common senses,
argument structures, constructions, and collocations should be introduced
when their grammatical, lexical, and contextual prerequisites become
available.

Translation equivalents must not be assumed to have identical semantic
ranges across languages. Cards should progressively reveal where apparently
corresponding words overlap, where they diverge, and how each language
conventionally divides meaning.

High-frequency words and constructions deserve coverage proportional not
only to their frequency as isolated forms but also to their lexical
productivity and semantic range. A versatile common verb may therefore
require many cards distributed across widely separated curriculum stages.

Idioms and fixed expressions form a continuum with ordinary collocation and
constructional usage. Curriculum units devoted to idioms, collocations,
phraseology, or natural usage are therefore consolidation and expansion
stages, not permission to present a few colourful examples and treat the
domain as complete.

Where a syllabus contains a dedicated idiom, collocation, construction, or
natural-usage stage, its completion standard should require systematic
breadth appropriate to the language and the maturity of the course.
Representative examples alone are insufficient when substantial
high-frequency phraseology remains untreated.

Mature lexical competence includes knowing not merely what combinations are
grammatically possible, but what speakers conventionally say in ordinary
contexts. Later broad-vocabulary and long-tail work should continue to add
senses, constructions, collocations, idioms, semantic distinctions, and
register information rather than treating phraseology as a completed
earlier topic.

12. LEXICAL ANCHORS

A lexical anchor is a word deliberately chosen to make a grammatical,
morphological, semantic, or orthographic pattern visible.

Anchors are teaching tools, not vocabulary limits.

A unit may name a small number of anchors while introducing additional
vocabulary whenever that vocabulary does not obscure the unit’s
objective.

Anchors should preferably be useful enough to remain valuable after
their instructional role is complete.

13. INTRODUCED, ESTABLISHED, AND REUSABLE

INTRODUCED means that a concept has appeared and begun to be
demonstrated.

ESTABLISHED means that the canonical curriculum has demonstrated the
concept sufficiently for later cards to assume its basic operation.

REUSABLE means that an established item or structure may appear
naturally in later cards without being the principal teaching objective.

These terms describe curriculum state, not learner mastery.

Introduction and establishment need not occur inside one sealed block.
A concept may receive enough controlled examples to make its pattern
intelligible, then continue toward establishment through later cards
whose principal objective is something else. Such later cards may
simultaneously reinforce previously introduced number, negation,
questions, tense, case, agreement, or other structures while introducing
new material.

Where two concepts have no genuine prerequisite relationship, the
curriculum need not invent one. Give each new concept enough controlled
exposure to keep its pattern clear, choose a sensible introduction order,
and allow subsequent integrated reuse to supply part of the coverage
needed for establishment.

A learner may personally struggle with an established concept. The
adaptive scheduler addresses that by increasing exposure. The canonical
curriculum does not retreat or reorder itself in response.

14. COMPLETION STANDARDS

Units do not have fixed card quotas unless a genuinely finite inventory
makes a count pedagogically meaningful.

Each unit instead has a completion standard: the minimum canonical
coverage needed before its principal concept can be treated as
established.

Completion standards should be based on demonstration of the pattern
across enough forms, contexts, contrasts, or lexical representatives to
support later reuse.

A simple unit may require only a few cards. A morphologically rich or
conceptually difficult unit may require many.

Variation in unit size is expected and desirable.

15. DELIBERATELY EXCLUDED MATERIAL

A syllabus unit should identify important material that is intentionally
postponed when doing so prevents future ambiguity.

Deliberately excluded material may include exceptions, competing
constructions, advanced meanings, alternate registers, orthographic
complications, or structures requiring later prerequisites.

Exclusion is temporary unless the material lies outside the intended
scope of the course.

This field prevents a simplified early rule from silently becoming the
permanent curriculum.

16. CARD STRATEGY

A syllabus may specify how a concept should actually be represented in
cards.

Card strategy may address such matters as:

-   whether to begin with isolated lexical forms or sentences;
-   how many contrasting forms should be introduced together;
-   when to vary person, number, case, tense, register, or word order;
-   whether translation ambiguity requires a controlled English prompt;
-   how script or input guidance should be handled;
-   when examples should become more natural and less controlled.

Card strategy should serve the linguistic objective rather than force
every language into the same exercise type.

17. SCRIPT, ORTHOGRAPHY, AND LITERACY

All languages follow the same fundamental card principle regardless of
writing system: the Target uses the natural conventional orthography of
the target language from its first appearance.

Polyglot does not create a separate transliteration curriculum or
require a romanized bootstrap before ordinary language begins. Cyrillic,
Arabic, Georgian, Devanagari, kana, kanji, and other writing systems are
encountered as part of learning the language itself, just as unfamiliar
sound-letter relationships in Latin-script languages are encountered
through ordinary vocabulary.

Difficulty is controlled by the choice and ordering of vocabulary and
structures, not by replacing the language's normal writing with a
learner transcription.

Writing-system knowledge therefore develops through repeated production
and recognition of real words and sentences. External references,
pronunciation audio, keyboard behaviour, alphabet tables, handwriting
practice, or other aids may support the learner when needed; Polyglot
need not encode every such prerequisite as permanent cards.

Where a language conventionally uses more than one script or a mixed
writing system, cards should use the ordinary contemporary orthographic
choice for the word or expression being taught. A familiar-looking or
pedagogically simpler spelling must not replace a conventional spelling
merely because the learner has not yet encountered its characters.

Script and literacy remain legitimate curricular subjects. Character
structure, spelling rules, orthographic alternations, readings,
punctuation, handwriting conventions, or other literacy systems may
receive explicit treatment when they constitute genuine knowledge of
the language. Such treatment should arise from authentic orthography
rather than from a preliminary transliteration deck.

Transliteration may appear as reference or input guidance where useful,
but it is not an accepted substitute Target for ordinary language and is
not itself treated as a language.
18. MORPHOLOGY AND PARADIGMS

Paradigms should be made visible without turning the curriculum into
table memorization.

A productive inflectional system should normally be introduced through
controlled examples that reveal its dimensions and regularities, then
mixed sufficiently to demonstrate productivity.

The curriculum should distinguish between productive morphology,
high-frequency irregularity, lexicalized forms, and patterns that are
primarily useful for recognition.

The appropriate dimensions are language-specific. Person, number,
gender, noun class, case, tense, aspect, mood, politeness,
evidentiality, definiteness, animacy, or other categories should appear
only where the language requires them.

19. SYNTAX AND INFORMATION STRUCTURE

The curriculum should teach the language’s actual organizing principles
rather than projecting English word order onto it.

If a language is best understood through verb-second structure,
topic-comment organization, argument omission, case marking, fixed
constituent order, clause fields, particles, agreement, or another
system, the syllabus should make that structure visible early enough to
prevent a misleading simpler model from becoming entrenched.

Simplification is useful when it is explicitly provisional and does not
create unnecessary later unlearning.

Discourse and information structure should receive explicit treatment
when they materially affect ordinary sentence formation or
interpretation.

20. SEMANTICS, PRAGMATICS, AND REGISTER

A mature curriculum must teach more than grammaticality.

It should progressively establish:

-   lexical meaning and semantic distinctions;
-   collocations and idioms;
-   ordinary pragmatic implications;
-   politeness and social distance;
-   formal, neutral, familiar, and informal registers where relevant;
-   spoken versus written tendencies;
-   generational or regional variation that an educated speaker commonly
    encounters.

Social or regional variants should be described rather than ranked as
inherently superior or defective.

Early stages may use a broadly neutral reference variety for coherence.
Major standard and commonly encountered variants should be introduced
later where relevant.

21. TRANSLATION AND PROMPT DESIGN

English prompts are tools for eliciting the intended target, not claims
that languages map word-for-word.

Prompts should be specific enough that the expected target is
unambiguous within the curriculum. Where English has several meanings
that the target language distinguishes, prompts may be disambiguated.

One accepted target string is preferred for a card. If two target forms
are both valid but represent different constructions, registers,
meanings, or curricular objectives, they should normally be taught as
separate cards or with prompts that distinguish them.

Exact matching, including meaningful diacritics and case, is
intentional.

Translation should not force unnatural explicit pronouns, word order,
tense, articles, or other English structures into the target language.

Every Target is intended to remain valid as a permanent member of the
deck. It should therefore represent natural mature-language usage for
the intended meaning and register, not a learner-simplified surrogate.
Curricular difficulty should be reduced by selecting simpler meanings,
vocabulary, and structures, not by making the target language
artificially explicit, orthographically simplified, or otherwise
unnatural.

22. INPUT GUIDANCE

Input guidance may be supplied where the learner needs practical
information about how to type a displayed target.

InputHint is optional and appears as assistance about input or reading;
it is not an alternative accepted answer and must not become a
transliteration curriculum.

For an input method that is ordinarily mediated by phonetic typing,
InputHint may record the practical keystroke reading needed to produce
the authentic Target. For example, a Japanese kanji Target may carry a
romaji InputHint even though the required answer remains Japanese
orthography.

Languages that use a direct target-script keyboard normally need no
InputHint merely because their script is unfamiliar. Input guidance may
be omitted or may disappear whenever it adds no practical value.
23. NATURALNESS VERSUS CONTROL

Early examples should be controlled enough that the intended pattern is
visible, but they should remain linguistically legitimate.

As concepts become established, examples should become increasingly
natural, lexically varied, and structurally integrated.

The curriculum should not preserve unnatural textbook language merely
because it is easy to parse.

Conversely, highly idiomatic natural language should not be introduced
so early that the learner cannot determine which part of the example is
teaching the intended concept.

24. SYNTHESIS UNITS

Synthesis units may follow clusters of related concepts.

Their purpose is not to introduce a new paradigm but to demonstrate that
previously separated structures can operate together under realistic
variation.

Synthesis cards should reduce cues that reveal which rule is being
tested and should broaden vocabulary where appropriate.

A synthesis unit is especially useful before later stages begin treating
the relevant grammar as unrestricted reusable background knowledge.

25. BROAD VOCABULARY AND THE LONG TAIL

Once the major grammatical scaffold is mature, vocabulary becomes
increasingly dominant.

Broad lexical development should cover ordinary adult domains such as
household life, food, relationships, health, work, education,
transportation, public places, nature, tools, technology, commerce,
government, science, culture, emotions, and abstract concepts, adapted
to the language and society.

Later expansion should include lower-frequency verbs, adjectives and
nouns; academic and professional vocabulary; broadly relevant
scientific, institutional, political, legal, economic and cultural
language; word formation; idiom; collocation; and fine semantic
distinctions.

The final long tail may include commonly encountered regional variation,
contemporary and generational usage, formal/literary vocabulary,
historical language still encountered in modern culture, and specialized
vocabulary of broad relevance.

26. EXISTING CARDS AND CURRICULUM RECONCILIATION

A new or amended syllabus does not invalidate earlier cards merely
because they were created before the specification existed.

When an existing deck and a new syllabus differ, perform curriculum
reconciliation:

1.  Compare existing cards with the syllabus.
2.  Determine which concepts are adequately established, partially
    covered, or not yet covered by the canonical card sequence.
3.  Preserve valid existing cards and IDs.
4.  Fill meaningful prerequisite or coverage gaps.
5.  Do not recreate material merely to make historical card order
    visually identical to the new syllabus.
6.  Converge toward the canonical syllabus.
7.  Once the historical frontier is reconciled, continue normally from
    the current canonical position.

Historical deviations may be documented in implementation notes or
progress records where useful.

Reconciliation should be a finite transition, not a permanent
alternative curriculum.

27. CURRICULUM PROGRESS AND THE MANIFEST

Progress does not belong inside the syllabus.

A Curriculum Manifest may record compact machine-readable state such as:

-   language;
-   syllabus version;
-   units established;
-   units partially covered;
-   current unit or frontier;
-   script/literacy position where relevant;
-   global card/ID checkpoint;
-   reconciliation notes.

The manifest is a checkpoint or cache, not the source of curriculum
truth. The syllabus defines the intended course; the CSV contains the
actual cards.

Where the current CSV is sufficient to reconstruct progress reliably,
the manifest need not become an unnecessary maintenance burden.

28. DOCUMENT ARCHITECTURE

The preferred durable document set is:

1.  Polyglot Curriculum Charter — system-wide governing principles.
2.  One living syllabus per language — the language-specific canonical
    roadmap.
3.  Curriculum Manifest — compact progress state where useful.
4.  Card CSV — actual cards and scheduling state.

Documents should use plain UTF-8 text with simple Markdown-like headings
unless another format has a clear practical advantage.

Syllabi and the Charter should have version numbers and concise
amendment histories.

29. LANGUAGE SYLLABUS UNIT TEMPLATE

The preferred unit template is:

LANGUAGE-XX — UNIT NAME

PREREQUISITES Earlier units or specific concepts assumed.

OBJECTIVE What the learner should acquire from this unit.

CORE PATTERN The grammatical, lexical, semantic, or orthographic pattern
being established, where applicable.

LEXICAL ANCHORS A small number of words deliberately chosen to make the
pattern visible. These are anchors, not a restriction on additional
vocabulary.

CARD STRATEGY How the concept should actually be taught through cards.

VOCABULARY DEVELOPMENT What kinds of new vocabulary can sensibly be
introduced alongside the unit.

DELIBERATELY EXCLUDED Exceptions, competing constructions, or later
concepts that would obscure the present lesson.

COMPLETION STANDARD What must have been demonstrated before the
curriculum can treat the concept as established.

LEADS TO Later units that depend upon this one.

IMPLEMENTATION NOTES Existing cards, historical deviations, script/input
issues, or other special considerations.

Not every field must be populated when irrelevant. Near-term units may
be specified in considerably more detail than distant long-tail units.

The template is a default organizational tool, not a requirement to
invent content for empty fields.

30. SYLLABUS DESIGN PROCEDURE

When designing a new language syllabus:

1.  Identify any script, orthographic, or input considerations that must develop alongside authentic language.
2.  Identify a very small reusable lexical seed. Test the default
    house/man/woman/child + big/small/good/bad seed and retain it wherever
    it works naturally.
2A. Stress-test the opening against established Polyglot sequences,
    especially the early French pacing model. Copy useful ordering closely
    where the target language permits; do not redesign for novelty.
3.  Determine the language’s true foundational organizing structures
    rather than importing categories from another language.
4.  Build a prerequisite graph from basic structures toward mature
    grammar.
5.  Introduce enough vocabulary continuously to prevent grammatical
    development from becoming lexically sterile.
6.  Plan longitudinal development of high-frequency polysemous words,
    constructions, collocations, and other phraseology.
7.  Insert synthesis points where formerly isolated systems need to
    operate together.
8.  Identify language-specific systems requiring dedicated treatment.
9.  Determine how literacy, orthography, register, pragmatics, and
    variation enter the course.
10.  Transition gradually from grammar-led organization toward broad
    lexical development.
10A. Determine whether the normal educated-adult completeness objective can be supported reliably. Documentation-limited status must be explicitly justified and declared in the syllabus; otherwise the language remains fully subject to the normal completeness objective.
11. Define the educated-adult and long-tail destination.
12. Compare the resulting syllabus with existing Polyglot syllabi for
    useful consistency.
13. Preserve shared structure where neutral; change anything that needs
    changing for the language.
14. Stress-test the syllabus against real example cards before treating
    it as stable.

31. CARD GENERATION PROCEDURE

When extending an established language deck:

1.  Read the governing Charter and current language syllabus.
2.  Determine current progress from the CSV and, where available/useful,
    the manifest.
3.  Reconcile historical gaps if the language predates its syllabus.
4.  Identify the next unmet canonical objective.
5.  Generate cards in prerequisite-aware order.
6.  Keep the intended new concept visible by controlling other novelty.
6A. For a new language, begin productive structure after only a small
    lexical seed. Do not satisfy broad vocabulary-development language in
    the syllabus by generating a long preliminary word list.
7.  Introduce useful vocabulary continuously and just in time, normally in
    small increments that can be reused immediately.
8.  Develop established high-frequency words longitudinally when new
    senses, constructions, collocations, or semantic contrasts become
    pedagogically available.
9.  Reuse established structures naturally.
10. Preserve stable IDs and CSV ordering.
11. Update progress records as appropriate; do not amend the syllabus
    merely because cards were generated.

32. QUALITY TESTS

A proposed card sequence should satisfy the following questions:

PREREQUISITE TEST Does the card require unexplained knowledge beyond the
intended new material?

PATTERN TEST Is the intended rule or distinction visible from the
surrounding sequence?

NATURALNESS TEST Is the target language legitimate and reasonably
natural for the intended register?

PERMANENCE TEST Would the Target still be worth retaining unchanged after
the learner becomes fully literate and advanced, or is it merely temporary
beginner scaffolding?

AMBIGUITY TEST Does the prompt identify the intended target sufficiently
for exact-answer grading?

VOCABULARY TEST Is lexical growth continuing without obscuring new
grammar?

BOOTSTRAP TEST Does productive language begin after a genuinely small
lexical seed, or has "bootstrap vocabulary" become an avoidable standalone
word list?

PACING-MODEL TEST Where an established Polyglot sequence already provides
a good opening, has it been reused or adapted unless the target language
gives a concrete reason not to?

LEXICAL-USAGE TEST Are important senses, constructions, collocations,
semantic boundaries, and phraseology developing with appropriate breadth,
rather than being reduced to isolated translation pairs or a few token
idioms?

INTERFERENCE TEST Are easily confused new items spaced appropriately
unless contrast is deliberate?

LANGUAGE-INTEGRITY TEST Is the language being taught according to its
own structure rather than an imported template?

REUSE TEST Are established concepts being allowed to recur naturally?

SHRINK-WRAPPED TEST Would this ordering still make pedagogical sense if
the learner’s scheduling history and performance data were erased?

LONG-TERM TEST Does the sequence build toward mature adult competence
rather than merely completing beginner textbook topics?

33. AMENDMENTS

The Charter should change only when a system-wide curriculum principle
changes.

A language syllabus should change only when that language’s canonical
curriculum changes.

Routine card generation and progress do not constitute amendments.

An amendment should:

-   identify the affected section or units;
-   state the pedagogical change;
-   explain whether it applies only to future cards or requires
    correction of existing cards;
-   increment the document version;
-   preserve a concise amendment record.

Changes should improve the curriculum rather than rewrite documents for
stylistic novelty.

34. GOVERNING PRIORITIES

When principles appear to compete, use the following order of judgment:

1.  Teach the target language accurately according to its own structure.
2.  Respect prerequisite knowledge and pedagogical clarity.
3.  Build toward useful, natural, mature language competence.
4.  Preserve coherent canonical sequencing.
5.  Maintain continuous vocabulary growth and longitudinal lexical-usage
    development.
6.  Preserve cross-language/document consistency where it does not
    conflict with the above.
7.  Prefer simplicity of implementation where pedagogically equivalent.

This ordering means that consistency is valuable but never a reason to
teach a language incorrectly or awkwardly.

35. AMENDMENT HISTORY

Version 0.1
- Initial Charter draft.
- Extracted from the curriculum architecture developed through French and
  German full syllabi and the Japanese stress test/full syllabus.
- Established the static-curriculum/adaptive-scheduler distinction,
  independent canonical curricula, prerequisite-aware ordering,
  rule-before-exception, controlled-example grammar teaching, continuous
  vocabulary growth, lexical anchors, completion standards, deliberate
  exclusions, language-specific script/literacy treatment, reconciliation,
  document architecture, syllabus design, card generation, and quality
  tests.

Version 0.2
- Adds a system-wide principle for lexical usage, polysemy, and phraseology.
- Makes lexical competence a continuous strand alongside vocabulary and
  grammar rather than a finite late-stage idiom topic.
- Requires longitudinal development of high-frequency polysemous words,
  including important senses, argument structures, constructions,
  collocations, semantic boundaries, idiomatic uses, and register.
- Clarifies that apparent translation equivalents need not share the same
  semantic range across languages.
- Requires dedicated idiom/collocation/natural-usage stages to achieve
  systematic breadth rather than token-example coverage.
- Adds lexical-usage considerations to syllabus design, card generation,
  quality testing, and governing priorities.
- Applies prospectively to future card generation and syllabus design.
  Existing valid cards do not require retrospective correction solely
  because of this amendment.


Version 0.3
- Establishes one writing-system policy for all languages: authentic
  conventional target-language orthography from first exposure.
- Removes transliteration/script bootstrap as a default curriculum stage.
- Treats script acquisition as something that develops through real
  vocabulary and sentences, with external aids permitted when useful.
- Preserves InputHint as optional post-answer input/reading guidance, not
  as an alternative Target or separate language.
- Establishes the permanence principle: permanent cards must contain
  natural mature-language Targets rather than learner-simplified output.
- Clarifies that difficulty is controlled by curriculum selection and
  sequencing, not by distorting orthography or natural expression.
- Repairs numbering defects introduced in version 0.2 without changing
  their substantive principles.


Version 0.4
- Adds the documentation-limited-language exception and requires it to be
  explicitly justified rather than inferred from speaker population,
  temporary resource scarcity, model confidence, or deck size.
- Preserves the normal educated-adult completeness objective as the
  default for languages not explicitly designated documentation-limited.

Version 0.5
- Replaces the potentially expansive "bootstrap useful vocabulary" model
  with a small-seed, immediate-structure, just-in-time vocabulary model.
- Establishes house/man/woman/child and big/small/good/bad as a reusable
  default eight-item seed where natural, not an immutable universal rule.
- Explicitly permits close reuse of successful existing card sequences;
  originality is not a curriculum objective.
- Identifies the early French deck as an approved pacing model while
  preserving language-specific structural integrity.
- Adds bootstrap and pacing-model checks to syllabus design and card
  generation.
- Applies prospectively to new generation and to deliberate rebuilds.
  Existing valid cards are not automatically invalidated by this amendment.


Version 0.6
- Makes explicit that mature-language units increasingly represent first
  introduction and longitudinal strands rather than serial mastery blocks.
- Requires prerequisite-aware introduction while permitting register,
  phraseology, broad lexical domains, educated-adult vocabulary, regional
  usage, and long-tail material to develop concurrently once introduced.
- Clarifies that numbered late units organize the curriculum without
  requiring one mature domain to be exhausted before another begins.

Version 0.7
- Clarifies that first introduction is not the same as establishment and
  that a concept may continue toward establishment through later
  integrated cards.
- Encourages double-duty cards that reinforce previously introduced
  structures while another concept supplies the principal novelty.
- Clarifies that the curriculum should not invent prerequisite
  relationships between genuinely independent concepts or require sealed
  practice blocks when longitudinal reuse can provide adequate coverage.
- Applies prospectively; existing valid cards require no correction solely
  because of this amendment.
