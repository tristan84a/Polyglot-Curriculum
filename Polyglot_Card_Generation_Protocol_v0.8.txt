POLYGLOT CARD GENERATION PROTOCOL

Version: 0.8
Status: Current
Encoding: UTF-8

1. PURPOSE

This protocol defines the practical workflow for creating and extending
Polyglot card CSVs.

It is intentionally lightweight. The Charter defines curriculum
principles; the language syllabus provides a path forward; existing
successful decks provide examples; discussion resolves meaningful
language-specific uncertainty. The protocol should not attempt to
automate every special case.

2. SOURCE PRIORITY

For an established language, use:

1. Polyglot Curriculum Charter.
2. Current language syllabus.
3. Current language CSV.
4. Curriculum Manifest when allocation or frontier information is useful.
5. Relevant established Polyglot decks as examples.
6. This protocol.

The CSV is authoritative for cards that actually exist. The syllabus is a
roadmap, not a requirement to reproduce every numbered unit as a sealed
block.

For a new language, inspect the Charter, relevant examples and what is
known about the target language before committing to a detailed sequence.
A complete syllabus need not be invented before the first safe prototype
if the user has explicitly chosen prototype-first development.

3. NEW-LANGUAGE WORKFLOW

The normal workflow is:

1. Discuss the target language's important early characteristics.
2. Inspect one or more useful Polyglot reference openings.
3. Propose a very small lexical/structural seed.
4. Build a short prototype, commonly somewhere around 30–60 cards when
   that is enough to reveal the opening.
5. Review what the prototype exposes: natural sentence structure,
   morphology, script burden, input needs, omissions, particles/cases,
   agreement, register, or other language-specific concerns.
6. Establish or refine the syllabus/path forward from that evidence.
7. Audit the prototype against the resulting path.
8. Continue in progressively larger batches as confidence increases.

The 30–60 range is a heuristic, not a quota. Some languages may reveal
their path sooner; others may need a larger experiment.

French, German and Japanese are strong current reference implementations
because their reworked openings demonstrate different solutions to the
same general problem. Other languages should also be consulted when they
offer a closer structural analogy.

Cards can help design the syllabus, but once the syllabus exists, the
syllabus governs the long-term path. Ordinary prerequisite-safe detours do
not require a syllabus amendment.

4. EXTENDING AN EXISTING LANGUAGE

Read the current syllabus and CSV, identify the useful next frontier, and
continue from there.

Do not mechanically search for the lowest numbered unfinished unit and
treat everything before the next number as a sealed prerequisite. Ask:

- What new structure or lexical strand is now ready?
- What previously introduced material still needs reinforcement?
- Can the same cards do both jobs naturally?
- Has card generation revealed a sensible local detour from the roadmap?

Historical or incidental later material may remain valid. Reconcile by
convergence rather than restarting the course.

5. SEED AND PACING

Begin new languages with a small reusable seed and move into productive
language quickly.

Test the common house/man/woman/child and big/small/good/bad seed where it
fits. Adapt it freely when the target language gives a reason. Japanese,
for example, showed the value of including a few concrete verbs in the
seed so that particles and verbal clauses could emerge immediately.

Add vocabulary just in time. Avoid long lexical front-loads. Avoid the
opposite error of demonstrating many grammar points indefinitely with the
same tiny vocabulary.

Existing Polyglot sequences may be copied closely when useful. Originality
is not a generation requirement.

6. INTRODUCTION AND REINFORCEMENT

Give a new pattern enough controlled exposure to become intelligible.
Then allow it to recur in cards whose principal novelty may be something
else.

Use double-duty cards deliberately. Questions can reinforce plurals;
negation can accompany new vocabulary; known tense can carry a new case;
known particles can support a new verb.

Do not mistake first appearance for establishment. Do not require every
concept to be established inside a self-contained block before proceeding.

Where two concepts have no genuine prerequisite relationship, choose a
clear introduction order and move on once the first is intelligible enough
that the second will not obscure it.

7. ORDER, SHUFFLING, AND DUPLICATES

CSV row order records canonical introduction order and should remain
pedagogically coherent.

It does not create conversational adjacency. Polyglot will eventually
shuffle available cards, so a card must not depend on "the previous card"
for its meaning or answer.

The scheduler supplies repeated exposure to a card. Do not create exact
duplicate cards for review. Reinforce a concept with varied examples,
contexts and combinations instead.

Order matters most for prerequisites and first introduction. Do not spend
time optimizing the relative order of independent cards that will
ultimately be shuffled.

8. PARADIGMS

Use systematic coverage when each cell teaches a meaningful contrast.
Avoid mechanically repeating an established paradigm with many lexical
stems.

Practical test:

If filling every cell teaches a new contrast, fill the table. If it merely
substitutes a new stem into an already-known table, sample and reuse.

This is a calibration rule, not a ban on paradigms.

9. COMPREHENSIBLE DISCOVERY

Not every form needs a dedicated introductory card before it can appear.
Once enough context exists for a learner to infer a new form safely,
comprehensible discovery is acceptable.

Do not use this as permission to stack several opaque new mechanisms into
one card. The intended novelty should remain recoverable.

10. MATURE CURRICULUM

As grammar matures, open new lexical, register, phraseology and discourse
strands relatively quickly and develop them longitudinally in parallel.

Do not make high card IDs artificially difficult. Later curriculum should
continue expanding educated-adult breadth, collocation, semantic precision
and register even when grammatical difficulty has largely plateaued.

11. CARD SCHEMA

Canonical language CSV:

ID,Language,EnglishPrompt,Target,InputHint,PartOfSpeech

Use UTF-8 with BOM when practical for Excel compatibility, comma
delimiting, standard CSV quoting and consistent Unicode normalization.

EnglishPrompt must be unique within the language under the current schema.
Exact duplicate cards are prohibited.

Each prompt has one intended Target. Case, diacritics, punctuation and
script are part of the answer where the language requires them. Improve
ambiguous prompts rather than relying on multiple accepted answers.

PartOfSpeech should use established Polyglot categories where useful.
InputHint is normally blank except where the intended input method
genuinely benefits from an intermediate representation. Language-specific
practice may be defined by the syllabus or current approved experiment.

12. NATURAL TARGET LANGUAGE

Targets should be natural for the intended meaning and register.

Do not mechanically preserve English pronouns, subjects, articles, word
order or other structure when the target language would normally express
the meaning differently.

Use ordinary contemporary orthography. Do not simplify normal spelling
merely because script is unfamiliar.

Lexical verbs normally use the target language's ordinary dictionary or
citation form. Grammar is normally taught through examples rather than
metalinguistic prompt cards.

13. IDS: DEVELOPMENT AND LIVE DECKS

IDs belong to the language block recorded in the Manifest.

During pre-production development, when cards have not yet accumulated
learner state, pedagogical cleanup may include reordering and renumbering
the draft sequence.

Once cards have entered real learner use, IDs become stable identities.
Assign new IDs monotonically and do not reuse an existing live ID for a
materially different card.

Ordinary corrections to a live card preserve its ID so scheduler state
remains attached to that card. If a proposed change would effectively
replace the card with different curriculum content, review it explicitly
rather than silently transferring learner state.

14. EXISTING-CARD CORRECTIONS

While extending a language, correct an existing card when a clear problem
becomes evident: factual error, unnatural neutral target, misleading
prompt, unnecessary explicitness, duplicate, or conflict with an
established convention.

Do not turn routine generation into a full retrospective audit unless
requested.

Report every correction to a pre-existing live card with its ID, old and
new content as relevant, and brief reason. Never silently alter live
curriculum.

15. VALIDATION

Before publishing a batch, verify at minimum:

1. IDs are unique and within the language's reserved block.
2. EnglishPrompt values are unique within the language.
3. No exact duplicate cards exist.
4. The six required columns are present in the correct order.
5. Targets use correct natural language, spelling, punctuation and script.
6. InputHint follows current language practice.
7. New cards have no unresolved prerequisite problem.
8. Cards stand independently when shuffled.
9. Existing-card changes are intentional and reported.
10. The stopping point is pedagogically defensible.

Do not reject a good batch merely because it took a reasonable detour from
the syllabus.

16. BATCH SIZE

Requested counts are approximate unless explicitly exact.

Prefer coherent stopping points over round numbers. Early experimental
batches should remain small enough for meaningful review. As a language's
path becomes clearer, larger batches are appropriate.

Do not pad a batch with redundant cards to hit a target count.

17. MANIFEST AND ARCHIVES

Update the Manifest when card extent or curriculum frontier materially
changes.

Git history is the normal archive for superseded development material.
Once a trial deck has yielded its useful lessons and a canonical sequence
has replaced it, do not keep duplicate archive CSVs in the Cards folder
unless there is a specific ongoing reason.

18. LEARNER STATE

Language curriculum CSVs contain authored curriculum only.

Operational app/export state may add:

Weight,Active,LastShown

When rebuilding a master/app file, preserve learner state by stable live
card ID. Initialize state only for genuinely new IDs. Do not attach old
learner state to a materially different card because an ID was reused.

Curriculum generation does not edit scheduler state.

19. WHEN TO DISCUSS

Stop and discuss rather than inventing a rule when a choice could
materially shape many later cards and the existing Charter, syllabus and
examples do not give a clear answer.

Good reasons for discussion include competing natural registers, a major
orthographic/input decision, uncertain treatment of a structural
distinction, or a choice that would commit the curriculum to a long
sequence.

Do not escalate trivial ordering questions or ordinary prerequisite-safe
detours. The purpose of discussion is judgment, not bureaucracy.

20. BATCH SUMMARY

For a substantive published batch, report:

- number of new cards and ID range;
- important structures or lexical strands advanced;
- significant decisions or unresolved questions;
- every correction to a pre-existing live card;
- the useful next frontier.

Do not overstate unit completion merely because a structure has appeared.

21. AMENDMENTS

This protocol is versioned. Amend it when the generation workflow changes,
not when a language merely advances.

Version 0.8 replaces increasingly elaborate special-case automation with a
simpler collaborative workflow: learn from existing examples, build and
review a small seed, let the target language reveal its needs, establish a
path forward, and expand in progressively larger batches. It formalizes
shuffle-independent cards, duplicate avoidance, paradigm calibration,
comprehensible discovery, development-versus-live ID handling, Git history
as the normal archive for retired prototypes, and explicit discussion at
meaningful design forks.

22. GOVERNING PRINCIPLE

Use the Charter for durable principles, the syllabus for direction, the
current CSV for reality, successful decks for examples, and discussion for
meaningful uncertainty. Generate the simplest coherent curriculum that
respects all five.
