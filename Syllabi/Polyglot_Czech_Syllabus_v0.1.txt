POLYGLOT CZECH SYLLABUS

Version 0.1
Status: Draft for curriculum review
Charter version: 0.3
Language: Czech
Reference variety: contemporary standard Czech, with commonly
encountered colloquial Czech introduced progressively

1. PURPOSE AND SCOPE

This syllabus defines the canonical curriculum sequence for Czech in
Polyglot.

It is governed by the Polyglot Curriculum Charter. It specifies what
Czech should teach and in what pedagogical order. It does not contain
learner scheduling state, weights, timestamps, or progress.

The curriculum begins with immediately reusable vocabulary and rapidly
builds the grammatical scaffold required for ordinary Czech. Vocabulary,
morphology, syntax, phraseology, register, and semantic precision then
expand together toward educated-adult competence.

Czech is taught as Czech, not as a transformation of Polish, Russian,
Ukrainian, Slovak, or English. Related Slavic languages may provide
useful comparative editorial models, but Czech usage and Czech
prerequisite structure determine this syllabus.

All Targets use authentic Czech orthography from first exposure. Czech
diacritics are required where standard orthography requires them.
InputHint is normally blank because Czech is entered directly with a
Czech keyboard; it is not a pronunciation or transliteration field.

2. CURRICULUM DESIGN PRINCIPLES

The Czech curriculum shall establish useful lexical material before
demanding large paradigms; introduce one major grammatical contrast at a
time where practical; teach distinctions through controlled natural
examples; preserve one intended Target per prompt wherever possible;
develop vocabulary continuously; introduce productive rules before
marginal exceptions; and teach aspect, case government, word order,
clitic placement, phraseology, semantics, and register longitudinally.

Every retained Target must remain suitable for permanent review. Natural
mature Czech is preferred over learner-simplified Czech.

3. WRITING SYSTEM AND INPUT

CS-01 — Czech orthography in ordinary vocabulary

Establish authentic Czech spelling through real vocabulary from the
first card.

Coverage: - á, č, ď, é, ě, í, ň, ó, ř, š, ť, ú, ů, ý, ž in ordinary
words; - normal capitalization and punctuation; - exact spelling as part
of lexical knowledge; - no alphabet or transliteration bootstrap.

InputHint normally remains blank.

4. STAGE I — BOOTSTRAP LEXICON AND MINIMAL SENTENCE FRAME

CS-02 — Core concrete nouns

High-frequency people, family, home, objects, food, animals, places,
time, nature, and body vocabulary. Prefer nouns reusable in later case
and agreement examples.

CS-03 — Core adjectives and oppositions

High-value descriptive pairs, colours, size, age, quality, temperature,
speed, ease, and physical properties.

CS-04 — Core verbs and infinitives

High-frequency equivalents of be, have, do/make, go, come, see, know,
want, need, like, eat, drink, read, write, work, live, speak,
understand, give, and take.

CS-05 — Personal pronouns and present identity

Já, ty, on, ona, ono, my, vy, oni/ony; present forms of být; predicate
nouns and adjectives; natural omission of recoverable subject pronouns.

CS-06 — Basic present-tense clauses

Useful regular and irregular present forms across persons and numbers,
using established vocabulary.

CS-07 — Negation

Verbal ne-, negative forms of být and mít, simple negative responses,
and natural negative concord.

CS-08 — Questions and answers

Ano, ne; kdo, co, kde, kam, odkud, kdy, proč, jak, kolik; yes/no and
information questions.

5. STAGE II — NOMINAL SYSTEM FOUNDATIONS

CS-09 — Gender and animacy

Masculine, feminine, neuter; masculine animate/inanimate distinctions;
common ending cues and useful exceptions.

CS-10 — Nominative agreement

Adjectives, demonstratives, predicate adjectives, and selected plural
patterns.

CS-11 — Accusative: direct objects

Main singular and plural patterns; masculine animate versus inanimate;
adjective and demonstrative agreement.

CS-12 — Locative: location and topic

V/ve, na, o with locative; noun/adjective forms and common stem
alternations.

CS-13 — Genitive

Possession, origin, absence, quantity, do, z/ze, od, bez, and common
lexical government. Do not import genitive-under-negation rules
mechanically from other Slavic languages.

CS-14 — Dative

Recipients, beneficiaries, experiencers, k/ke, and common
dative-governing verbs and predicates.

CS-15 — Instrumental

S/se accompaniment, means/instrument, selected complements, and full
agreement.

CS-16 — Vocative

Productive direct-address forms for names, kinship terms, social nouns,
and common irregular patterns.

CS-17 — Case synthesis I

Mix all seven cases in natural short sentences without announcing which
case is required.

6. STAGE III — PRESENT VERBS, REFLEXIVES, MODALS

CS-18 — Present conjugation patterns

Productive present patterns, high-frequency irregulars, stem
alternations, person/number endings, and pronoun omission.

CS-19 — Reflexive se and si

Inherent reflexives, reflexive and reciprocal meanings, dative si, and
initial unstressed placement.

CS-20 — Modal verbs and infinitives

Moci, muset, chtít, smět and other common modal constructions with
infinitives.

CS-21 — Imperative

Informal singular, plural/formal, irregular imperatives, negative
commands, and polite alternatives.

CS-22 — Possession and svůj

Possessive pronouns, mít, genitive possession where natural, and
especially svůj for subject-coreferential possession.

7. STAGE IV — PAST, FUTURE, ASPECT, AND MOTION

CS-23 — Past tense foundations

L-participle, gender/number agreement, first/second-person auxiliaries,
third-person past without overt auxiliary, negation.

CS-24 — Past auxiliaries and clitic behavior

Jsem, jsi, jsme, jste as unstressed elements; interaction with se/si and
pronoun clitics; neutral second-position tendencies.

CS-25 — Imperfective and perfective aspect

Completed event versus process/repetition; common aspect pairs;
prefixal/suffixal relations; aspect as lexical grammar rather than a
tense.

CS-26 — Imperfective future

Budu + infinitive across persons, negation, and questions.

CS-27 — Perfective future

Present-form morphology with future reference and contrast with
imperfective analytic future.

CS-28 — Motion verb foundations

Jít/chodit, jet/jezdit and directional versus habitual/non-directional
motion.

CS-29 — Prefixed motion verbs

High-frequency formations such as přijít, odejít, vejít, vyjít, přijet,
odjet; direction, aspect, and case government.

CS-30 — Aspect synthesis

Past/present/future contexts; process, repetition, result, completion,
sequence, and lexical exceptions.

8. STAGE V — PRONOUNS, CLITICS, NUMERALS

CS-31 — Personal pronouns across cases

Long and clitic forms, prepositional n- forms, emphasis, dative and
accusative pronouns.

CS-32 — Clitic clusters

Se/si, short pronouns, past auxiliaries, later conditional forms, and
neutral ordering.

CS-33 — Demonstratives and indefinites

Ten/ta/to, tento, někdo/něco, nikdo/nic, nějaký/žádný and related
adverbs; negative concord.

CS-34 — Interrogative and relative pronouns

Kdo, co, který, jaký, čí across cases and meanings.

CS-35 — Cardinal numbers

Common numbers, compound numerals, 2–4 versus 5+ noun government,
quantities.

CS-36 — Numeral syntax

Case behavior, quantified noun phrases, predicate agreement,
animate/person forms, approximate quantities.

CS-37 — Ordinals, dates, time, age, measurement

Ordinals, calendar dates, clock time, age, prices, dimensions, years,
centuries, fractions and decimals.

9. STAGE VI — ADJECTIVES, ADVERBS, COMPARISON

CS-38 — Adjective declension consolidation

Hard and soft adjective paradigms and high-value irregular/possessive
patterns across cases.

CS-39 — Comparison

Comparatives, superlatives, irregular common forms, než, and adverb
comparison.

CS-40 — Adverbs

Productive adjective-to-adverb relations, lexical adverbs, degree and
sentence adverbs.

CS-41 — Predicate and lexical adjective patterns

Productive contemporary patterns only; avoid treating historically
broader short-form systems as ordinary modern productive grammar.

10. STAGE VII — WORD ORDER AND INFORMATION STRUCTURE

CS-42 — Neutral Czech word order

Common SVO baseline, subject omission, topic-comment organization,
old/new information, neutral object placement.

CS-43 — Second-position clitics

Clitic placement after the first syntactic constituent;
auxiliary/reflexive/pronominal clusters; conjunction and clause effects.

CS-44 — Focus and marked order

Contrastive fronting, sentence-final focus, emphatic pronouns, and
discourse-driven alternatives.

CS-45 — Word-order synthesis

Natural sentences combining case, clitics, aspect, pronoun omission, and
information structure.

11. STAGE VIII — CONDITIONALS, PASSIVES, IMPERSONALS

CS-46 — Conditional bych

Bych, bys, by, bychom, byste, by with l-participles, agreement, clitic
placement, politeness and hypotheticals.

CS-47 — Kdyby and counterfactual meanings

Unreal present/future, unreal past, condition/result ordering.

CS-48 — Participial passive

Být + passive participle, agreement, agents where natural, and
comparison with preferred active/reflexive alternatives.

CS-49 — Reflexive passive and impersonal se

Institutional/general statements, signs, instructions, and contrast with
true reflexive meanings.

CS-50 — Other impersonal constructions

Weather, experiencers, necessity, general human subjects, and impersonal
predicates.

12. STAGE IX — SUBORDINATION

CS-51 — Basic conjunctions

A, ale, nebo, protože, proto, takže, když, jestli; coordination and
subordination.

CS-52 — Complement clauses

Že, jestli/zda; statements and questions after verbs of knowing,
thinking, saying, hoping, seeing, and wanting.

CS-53 — Relative clauses

Který across gender/number/case; kdo/co where appropriate; prepositional
relatives.

CS-54 — Temporal clauses

Když, až, než, dokud, jakmile; simultaneity, sequence, and aspect.

CS-55 — Purpose, cause, result, concession

Aby, protože, proto, takže, i když/přestože and common alternatives.

CS-56 — Indirect speech

Reported statements/questions and Czech deictic/reference patterns
without importing English sequence-of-tense rules.

13. STAGE X — WORD FORMATION

CS-57 — Verbal prefixes

Productive meanings, aspectual effects, lexicalized meanings, and limits
of predictability.

CS-58 — Secondary imperfectives and suffixation

Productive imperfectivization, repeated/ongoing meanings, derivational
families.

CS-59 — Noun derivation

Professions/people, action/result nouns, abstracts, productive
diminutives, and lexicalized formations.

CS-60 — Adjective derivation

Relational, origin/material, possessive, negative, and evaluative
formations.

CS-61 — Compounds and international vocabulary

Native formations, compounds, learned vocabulary, and Czech adaptation
of borrowings.

14. STAGE XI — GOVERNMENT, SEMANTICS, COLLOCATION

CS-62 — Verb government

Case/preposition requirements, especially where Czech differs from
English.

CS-63 — Adjective and noun government

Required cases/prepositions and common semantic contrasts.

CS-64 — Preposition networks

V/ve, na, do, z/ze, od, k/ke, s/se, u, o, po, pro, přes, před, za, mezi,
nad, pod in spatial and abstract uses.

CS-65 — Polysemy and lexical boundaries

Separate Czech meanings that collapse into one English gloss by using
disambiguated prompts.

CS-66 — Collocations

Verb+noun, adjective+noun, adverb+adjective, institutional and everyday
fixed preferences.

15. STAGE XII — STANDARD, SPOKEN, AND COMMON CZECH

CS-67 — Standard versus ordinary spoken Czech

Neutral spoken variants and high-frequency Common Czech forms with their
standard equivalents and contexts.

CS-68 — Common Czech morphology

Widely encountered adjective endings, ý/ej correspondences, common
leveling and frequent colloquial forms where genuinely widespread.

These are sociolinguistic variants, not errors and not universal Czech.

CS-69 — Spoken syntax and particles

Discourse particles, fillers, softeners, emphasis, ellipsis, and
response formulas.

CS-70 — Formal and written Czech

Formal correspondence, public/institutional language, written
tendencies, polite address and titles.

CS-71 — Register synthesis

Parallel meanings in neutral, colloquial/informal, and formal contexts
where Czech genuinely distinguishes them.

16. STAGE XIII — PHRASEOLOGY AND PRAGMATICS

CS-72 — Everyday formulas

Greetings, leave-taking, thanks, apologies, requests, invitations,
congratulations, condolences, service and telephone encounters.

CS-73 — Politeness and social distance

Ty/vy, titles, surnames, mitigation, conditional politeness, address
conventions.

CS-74 — Idioms and fixed expressions

Broadly current high-frequency idioms.

CS-75 — Discourse markers

Sequencing, correction, hesitation, contrast, conclusion, stance,
inference.

CS-76 — Pragmatic implication

Indirect requests, understatement, agreement/disagreement, softening and
intensification.

17. STAGE XIV — EXPANDED ADULT VOCABULARY

CS-77 — Home, food, clothing, daily life

CS-78 — Relationships, personality, emotions

CS-79 — Health, body, medicine, care

CS-80 — Work, occupations, organizations, business

CS-81 — Education, research, intellectual life

CS-82 — Transportation, travel, geography, public space

CS-83 — Technology, computing, media, communication

CS-84 — Nature, environment, agriculture, weather

CS-85 — Commerce, money, banking, consumer life

CS-86 — Government, law, administration, civic life

CS-87 — Science, mathematics, engineering, measurement

CS-88 — Arts, literature, music, religion, cultural life

18. STAGE XV — ADVANCED GRAMMAR AND STYLE

CS-89 — Participles and verbal adjectives

Passive participles, commonly encountered active/adjectival formations,
lexicalization, formal uses.

CS-90 — Transgressives (přechodníky)

Recognition of forms encountered in literature, historical prose, and
stylized writing. Active production is secondary because these are not
ordinary neutral conversational Czech.

CS-91 — Nominal style and information density

Deverbal nouns, institutional nominalization, compressed written syntax.

CS-92 — Advanced clause linkage

Nuanced concession, condition, cause, consequence, restriction,
comparison, formal connectives.

CS-93 — Advanced word order and style

Discourse-sensitive ordering, rhythm, clitic placement, marked literary
order, stylistic alternatives.

19. STAGE XVI — CZECH ORTHOGRAPHIC AND MORPHOPHONOLOGICAL DEPTH

CS-94 — Vowel length and alternation

Long/short contrasts, morphological/derivational alternations, ú versus
ů in real vocabulary.

CS-95 — ě and consonant behavior

Dě/tě/ně, bě/pě/vě/mě and morphology involving ě.

CS-96 — Palatalization and stem alternations

K/c, h/z, ch/š and related alternations in cases, vocative, and
derivation.

CS-97 — i/y and agreement-sensitive spelling

Lexical conventions, hard/soft consonant patterns, past plural agreement
spelling.

CS-98 — mě/mně and high-value spelling distinctions

Pronoun forms and other frequent educated-writing contrasts.

CS-99 — Capitalization, punctuation, formal orthography

Proper names, nationalities/languages, institutions, subordinate-clause
commas, direct speech, abbreviations, numbers and dates.

20. STAGE XVII — REGIONAL AND CONTACT-LANGUAGE AWARENESS

CS-100 — Major regional variation

Broadly encountered Bohemian, Moravian, and Silesian differences, with
standard correspondences.

CS-101 — Czech and Slovak contact

High-frequency cognates and false friends, obvious morphological
correspondences, differing lexical choices, and useful recognition of
common Slovak forms.

This is not a Slovak mini-course. Slovak forms are not accepted Czech
Targets.

CS-102 — Czech and Polish comparison

Cognates, false friends, superficially similar morphology with different
usage, and useful spelling/lexical correspondences.

CS-103 — German and international contact vocabulary

Established German-origin vocabulary, international formations,
colloquial borrowings, register and connotation.

21. STAGE XVIII — EDUCATED-ADULT LANGUAGE

CS-104 — Abstract and conceptual vocabulary

Cause, evidence, possibility, responsibility, tendency, consequence,
purpose, method, structure, value, degree, change, comparison.

CS-105 — Argument and explanation

Positions, reasons, qualification, counterargument, uncertainty,
evidence, inference, conclusion.

CS-106 — Academic and professional prose

Research, reports, technical explanation, administration, presentations,
professional correspondence.

CS-107 — News and public affairs

Institutions, government, economics, courts, international affairs,
policy, journalistic conventions.

CS-108 — Literature and cultural reference

Common literary vocabulary, narrative conventions, figurative language,
historically persistent forms, cultural expressions.

22. STAGE XIX — SEMANTIC AND PHRASEOLOGICAL LONG TAIL

CS-109 — Near-synonyms

Distinguish register, collocation, intensity, viewpoint and semantic
scope.

CS-110 — Verb families and aspectual nuance

Prefixed families, secondary imperfectives, habitual/iterative meanings,
lexicalized aspect, non-straightforward pairs.

CS-111 — Fine motion and spatial distinctions

Additional motion verbs, carrying/leading/driving distinctions, path,
direction and figurative extensions.

CS-112 — Evaluative and emotional nuance

Degrees of approval/disapproval, emotion, interpersonal stance,
colloquial/formal evaluation.

CS-113 — Advanced idiom

Widely recognized fixed expressions, conventional metaphors, and
culturally current proverbs.

25. CROSS-CURRICULAR REQUIREMENTS

Vocabulary, case government, aspect, word order, clitic placement,
register, phraseology, exact orthography, and naturalness remain
longitudinal requirements after their first dedicated units.

Later cards should increasingly resemble unrestricted adult Czech while
retaining sufficiently specific English prompts.

26. CARD AUTHORING NOTES

EnglishPrompt: Use wording specific enough to elicit one intended Czech
Target. Parenthetical disambiguation may specify lexical sense, aspect,
case context, register, or construction when necessary.

Target: Use one natural Czech string. Do not distort Czech to mirror
English structure.

InputHint: Normally blank. Do not use it for pronunciation, ASCII
spelling, or stripped diacritics.

Variants: When forms differ meaningfully by register, region,
construction, or curricular objective, use separate cards with
distinguishing prompts rather than multiple accepted Targets.

27. COMPLETION PHILOSOPHY

A unit is established when its productive core is sufficiently
represented that later cards can rely on it as background knowledge.
Completion does not mean every lexical item using that structure has
appeared.

Early units require enough varied examples to establish a pattern beyond
memorized phrases. Later synthesis and lexical expansion repeatedly
reactivate earlier grammar.

The canonical curriculum is static in design. Learner performance
affects scheduling, not canonical order.

28. EDITORIAL REVIEW NOTES FOR VERSION 0.1
