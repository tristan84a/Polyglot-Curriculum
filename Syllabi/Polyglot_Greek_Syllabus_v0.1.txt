POLYGLOT GREEK SYLLABUS

Version: 0.1
Status: Current
Charter version: 0.8
Encoding: UTF-8

PURPOSE

This syllabus defines the canonical pedagogical path for Modern Greek in
Polyglot. It is informed by the initial 100-card Greek seed and by successful
patterns in the established Polyglot decks. It is a roadmap rather than a
turn-by-turn transcript. Useful structures may appear before their formal
unit when they are comprehensible, and earlier structures may continue to be
established longitudinally through later cards.

The initial reference variety is ordinary contemporary Standard Modern Greek.
Ancient, Katharevousa, Cypriot and other regional or historical varieties are
not treated as interchangeable with the modern standard, but important
variation may be introduced later when useful.

COURSE-WIDE GREEK PRINCIPLES

1. Use ordinary monotonic Greek orthography from the first card. Greek script
   is the Target; transliteration is not a parallel curriculum.
2. Stress marks are part of exact spelling and should be preserved.
3. Greek question punctuation uses the semicolon-shaped question mark (;).
   Cards must use conventional Greek punctuation rather than English ?.
4. Noun gender and article form are persistent lexical/grammatical properties.
   Teach masculine, feminine and neuter through useful noun phrases and
   agreement rather than as detached labels.
5. Greek normally allows subject pronouns to be omitted. Pronoun cards teach
   the pronouns; ordinary finite clauses should omit them when natural.
6. Articles are central to Greek noun phrases. Definite articles, indefinite
   forms, case and agreement should develop through controlled examples.
7. Case is taught through function and visible article/noun contrasts. Modern
   Greek nominative, accusative, genitive and vocative develop longitudinally;
   do not begin with a complete declension table.
8. Verbal person/number morphology is productive and should become visible
   early. Paradigms are worth systematic coverage when the endings themselves
   teach a contrast.
9. Greek aspect is foundational. Present/imperfective and aorist/perfective
   stems, the particle να, future θα, past tense and imperatives must be
   developed as interacting systems rather than translated mechanically from
   English tense labels.
10. The 100-card seed deliberately previews να + dependent verb forms through
    θέλω. This is not premature material to remove; later cards should unpack
    and reinforce the aspectual and subordinate-verb system.
11. Negation distinguishes δεν in ordinary indicative clauses from μη(ν) in
    relevant non-indicative and prohibitive environments. Introduce the
    contrast through natural constructions.
12. Weak object pronouns/clitics are a major Greek system. Introduce them
    after basic direct objects are intelligible, then revisit their position
    with finite verbs, να, θα, imperatives and combinations.
13. Vocabulary grows just in time. Do not postpone useful ordinary language
    merely to exhaust an inflectional table.
14. Spoken-standard alternatives such as μιλάω/μιλώ or μιλάνε/μιλούν should
    not be multiplied into competing exact-match answers on one card. Choose
    a natural form consistently for a card and introduce important variants
    explicitly later where useful.

THE EMPIRICAL OPENING: CARDS 1–100

The seed establishes:
- the shared house/man/woman/child and big/small/good/bad anchors;
- nominative definite and indefinite noun phrases;
- masculine/feminine/neuter adjective agreement;
- personal pronouns followed by natural pro-drop finite clauses;
- present είμαι;
- early present morphology with μιλάω and δουλεύω;
- δεν negation and punctuation-only polar questions;
- πού, τι and ποιος;
- έχω possession;
- first visible accusative article contrasts through βλέπω;
- θέλω + να with dependent verb forms, deliberately previewing the Greek
  aspect/subordination system;
- ναι/όχι and short conversational responses.

The opening should be preserved as evidence rather than rewritten to make
every later system appear in textbook order.

INITIAL STRUCTURAL ROADMAP

GR-01 — Core nouns, articles and gender
Continue high-frequency concrete nouns with ο/η/το and useful ένας/μια/ένα
phrases. Reinforce gender lexically and through agreement.

GR-02 — Adjective agreement
Develop masculine, feminine, neuter and plural agreement with common
adjectives. Introduce productive patterns before less predictable classes.

GR-03 — Personal reference and pro-drop
Establish εγώ, εσύ, αυτός/αυτή/αυτό, εμείς, εσείς, αυτοί/αυτές/αυτά, while
modeling omission of recoverable subjects in ordinary clauses. Develop
formal/plural εσείς naturally.

GR-04 — Present είμαι
Identity, classification, description and location with the full useful
person/number system. Avoid importing English article usage mechanically
into predicate nouns.

GR-05 — Productive present morphology
Develop common active present patterns across useful verbs. Contrast
person/number endings systematically where they carry new information,
then sample additional verbs rather than repeating whole paradigms
mechanically.

GR-06 — Negation and polar questions
Establish δεν with indicative clauses and conventional Greek question
punctuation. Expand short answers and natural conversational responses.

GR-07 — Content questions
Develop ποιος, τι, πού, πότε, πώς, γιατί, πόσο and inflected interrogative
forms where case/gender requires them.

GR-08 — έχω and possession
Develop έχω in ordinary possession and related high-frequency expressions.
Later contrast possessive clitics and genitive noun phrases.

GR-09 — Accusative direct objects
Use definite articles and nouns to establish τον/τη(ν)/το and plural
accusative forms. Develop article-final ν according to contemporary
orthographic/phonological conventions rather than treating it as a separate
case ending.

GR-10 — Nominative and accusative synthesis
Mix subjects, direct objects, questions, negation, adjective agreement and
fresh vocabulary so case marking becomes ordinary background structure.

GR-11 — Plural noun phrases
Develop common noun plural patterns and plural article/adjective agreement.
Irregular high-frequency plurals may enter as lexical families.

GR-12 — Genitive: possession and relation
Introduce μου, σου, του, της, μας, σας, τους and simple genitive noun phrases
through possession and relationship. Keep clitic possessives distinct from
English possessive-adjective assumptions.

GR-13 — Basic prepositions and location
Develop σε, από, με, για and common contractions such as στο/στη/στον/στην
through location, direction, source, accompaniment and purpose. Lexical
choice and case behavior should be learned in natural phrases.

GR-14 — Demonstratives and determiners
Develop αυτός/αυτή/αυτό as demonstratives with the article where required,
along with εκείνος and other high-frequency determiners. Keep demonstrative
pronoun uses distinct from attributive noun-phrase uses.

VERBAL AND ASPECTUAL SPINE

GR-15 — να as a core clause particle
Return explicitly to the seed's θέλω να... constructions. Establish να +
dependent verb forms for wants, ability/necessity and other common
complements without forcing an English infinitive analysis onto Greek.

GR-16 — Imperfective versus perfective stem
Introduce transparent verb families where present/imperfective and
aorist/perfective stems make the contrast visible. Use contexts that
distinguish ongoing/repeated activity from bounded completion.

GR-17 — Simple future with θα
Develop θα with imperfective and perfective dependent forms: ongoing or
repeated future versus single/bounded future. Reuse the same aspect families
from GR-16.

GR-18 — Aorist active
Introduce completed past events with common regular patterns, then frequent
irregular aorists. Person/number morphology should be sufficiently
systematic to expose the paradigm without multiplying lexical stems
unnecessarily.

GR-19 — Imperfect
Develop ongoing, habitual and background past. Contrast with aorist in
controlled narratives and repeated everyday contexts.

GR-20 — Past/future/aspect synthesis
Interleave present, imperfect, aorist and both future aspect choices with
clear contextual prompts. Do not reduce the system to English
present/past/future equivalence.

GR-21 — Present perfect and pluperfect
Introduce έχω + invariant perfective form in ordinary contemporary uses,
then pluperfect and future perfect where frequency justifies them. Compare
with aorist pragmatically rather than claiming a one-to-one English mapping.

GR-22 — Imperatives
Develop positive imperatives with appropriate aspect and number. Negative
commands use μη(ν) plus the relevant non-indicative construction rather
than a mechanically negated positive imperative.

GR-23 — Modal and impersonal predicates
Develop μπορώ, πρέπει, χρειάζεται, είναι δυνατόν and other common modal or
impersonal frames with να clauses.

OBJECTS, CLITICS, AND VOICE

GR-24 — Weak direct-object pronouns
Introduce με, σε, τον, τη(ν), το, μας, σας, τους, τις, τα as clitics in
controlled finite clauses. Keep them distinct from strong pronouns.

GR-25 — Weak indirect/genitive pronouns
Develop μου, σου, του, της, μας, σας, τους as indirect/genitive clitics in
natural recipient, possession and experiencer constructions.

GR-26 — Clitic position
Contrast preverbal clitics with their behavior around να and θα, and later
with positive imperatives. Introduce combinations only after single clitics
are stable enough to remain intelligible.

GR-27 — Strong pronouns and emphasis
Develop εμένα, εσένα and other strong forms after the clitic system has a
clear functional base. Use contrastive/emphatic contexts rather than
treating them as interchangeable duplicates.

GR-28 — Mediopassive morphology
Introduce common present mediopassive forms and verbs whose ordinary lexical
form is mediopassive. Separate productive voice contrasts from lexically
deponent behavior.

GR-29 — Passive and reflexive/reciprocal meanings
Develop passive event structure, reflexive interpretations and reciprocal
expressions through natural high-frequency examples.

NOUN PHRASE DEVELOPMENT

GR-30 — Accusative across noun classes
Widen singular/plural accusative endings and article/adjective agreement
across masculine, feminine and neuter classes.

GR-31 — Genitive across noun classes
Develop productive singular and plural genitive patterns, common stress
shifts and high-frequency irregular forms through possession, source,
quantity and governed expressions.

GR-32 — Vocative
Introduce common masculine/feminine vocative patterns in direct address.
Treat productive endings and frequent irregulars according to actual usage.

GR-33 — Adjective declension
Broaden adjective classes through nominative, accusative and genitive
agreement. Avoid giant detached tables; use noun phrases and sentences.

GR-34 — Comparison
Develop πιο + adjective/adverb, από and superlative uses with the definite
article, then lexical comparatives where ordinary.

GR-35 — Numbers and quantity
Cardinals, ordinals, time, dates, age, prices and quantity expressions.
Develop noun behavior after numerals through actual modern usage rather than
importing Ancient Greek expectations.

GR-36 — Possessives and reflexive possession
Develop possessive clitics, δικός μου and related emphatic/contrastive
possessive constructions.

CLAUSE STRUCTURE

GR-37 — Coordination
και, αλλά, ή, ούτε and common discourse coordinators in increasingly varied
sentences.

GR-38 — ότι/πως content clauses
Develop finite complement clauses after verbs of saying, knowing, thinking
and perception, contrasting them with να complements where meaning differs.

GR-39 — Relative clauses
Introduce που as the central relative marker, then contexts requiring
additional resumptive or prepositional material.

GR-40 — Temporal clauses
όταν, πριν, αφού, ενώ, μόλις and aspect/tense choices in ordinary temporal
relations.

GR-41 — Cause, purpose and result
γιατί, επειδή, για να, ώστε and related constructions with indicative or
να clauses as appropriate.

GR-42 — Conditionals
Develop αν clauses from open present/future conditions through unreal and
counterfactual patterns. Introduce θα and past morphology in the combinations
actually used by Modern Greek.

GR-43 — Indefinites and negatives
κάποιος, κάτι, κανείς/κανένας, τίποτα, ποτέ and Greek negative-concord
patterns. Contrast polarity-sensitive meanings through complete clauses.

GR-44 — Word order and information structure
Greek word order is flexible but not free. Develop neutral orders, topical
subjects/objects, focus, clitic doubling and marked orders through contexts
where information structure makes the choice meaningful.

GR-45 — Questions beyond the basics
Develop alternative questions, embedded questions, question-word case forms,
particles and pragmatic question patterns.

LEXICAL AND DERIVATIONAL DEVELOPMENT

GR-46 — Everyday high-frequency verb families
Build broad lexical coverage while preserving aspectual partners/stems as
families rather than isolated translations.

GR-47 — Prefixes and lexical aspect
Develop productive and semi-productive prefixal relationships cautiously.
Do not imply that every prefixed verb has a compositionally predictable
English equivalent.

GR-48 — Noun and adjective derivation
Develop common suffixes, agent nouns, abstract nouns, diminutives,
augmentatives and adjective formation through useful lexical families.

GR-49 — Adverbs and discourse markers
Expand manner, time, degree, stance and connective adverbs from conversational
through educated written use.

GR-50 — Collocation and argument structure
Revisit common verbs and adjectives with their natural prepositions, cases,
clitics and complement types. Translation-equivalent words should acquire
their Greek-specific combinatorial behavior.

MATURE MODERN GREEK

GR-51 — Conversation and pragmatics
Greetings, requests, politeness, agreement/disagreement, hesitation,
repair, turn-taking and ordinary spoken routines. Teach pronoun use and
omission as pragmatic choices where relevant.

GR-52 — Register
Develop neutral spoken/written Greek, colloquial reductions and more formal
educated usage. Do not resurrect Katharevousa as the productive norm, but
teach common formal or fossilized forms an educated adult encounters.

GR-53 — Standard variation
Introduce important alternative standard forms and high-frequency regional
or Cyprus-related variation where it materially improves comprehension.
Label variants when exact matching would otherwise create ambiguity.

GR-54 — Learned vocabulary and the ancient inheritance
Modern Greek contains extensive inherited and learned morphology and
vocabulary. Develop common learned formations, doublets and formal lexical
patterns as modern vocabulary, with historical explanation only where it
clarifies present-day use.

GR-55 — Media, institutions and public life
Government, education, health, law, economics, work, technology, news and
ordinary institutional language.

GR-56 — Arts, science and educated discourse
Develop broad educated-adult vocabulary, abstract nouns, technical-general
terms, argumentation, description and interpretation.

GR-57 — Idiom and phraseology
Common idioms, fixed expressions, light-verb constructions, collocations
and conventional figurative language.

GR-58 — Extended syntax and discourse
Participial/fossilized structures that remain productive or common,
nominalization, complex embedding, cohesion, reference tracking and
multi-clause written prose.

GR-59 — Literary and historical comprehension
Only after the contemporary system is mature, introduce high-value literary,
older or conservative forms that an educated modern reader is likely to
encounter. Keep Ancient Greek and historical stages outside the ordinary
production target unless explicitly identified.

GR-60 — Educated-adult long tail
Continue vocabulary, collocation, semantic distinctions, register and
specialized-general knowledge without manufacturing artificial grammatical
difficulty merely because card IDs are high.

CARD-SPECIFIC RULES

1. EnglishPrompt must remain unique and have one intended Greek Target.
2. Use Greek punctuation, including ; for questions.
3. Use monotonic accents consistently; preserve diaeresis where required.
4. InputHint is normally blank. The learner should use a Greek keyboard
   rather than depend on romanization.
5. Subject pronouns should not be retained merely to mirror English.
6. When a Greek form has common competing variants, choose one natural
   production target and introduce the other later with a disambiguated
   prompt if it deserves active production.
7. Do not create cards whose only purpose is naming grammatical cases,
   tenses or aspects. Teach the systems through language.
8. Article + noun and adjective + noun examples should expose gender/case
   naturally. Avoid redundant gender-label cards when the phrase itself
   supplies the evidence.
9. να and θα constructions should be prompted semantically. Do not call
   dependent perfective forms an English-style infinitive.
10. Aspect-sensitive English prompts should contain enough context to select
    one intended Greek form.
11. Clitic cards must make person, number, gender and grammatical role clear
    enough for exact matching.
12. Greek spelling conventions and natural contemporary usage outrank
    etymological symmetry or analogy with Ancient Greek.

DEVELOPMENT POLICY

The first 100 cards are the empirical opening and are now governed by this
syllabus. Audit them for genuine errors, but do not rewrite them simply to
make the roadmap look more linear.

The next expansion should reinforce article/adjective agreement and present
morphology while opening plural noun phrases, broader accusative contrasts,
σε and other basic prepositions, genitive possessives, and a more explicit
return to να and aspect. Past and future language need not wait for every
noun paradigm to be complete.

As confidence grows, batch sizes may increase. Exact duplicates remain
prohibited; longitudinal reinforcement should use varied contexts.

AMENDMENT NOTE

Version 0.1 is the first Greek syllabus. It was written after a 100-card
prototype rather than before it. The prototype demonstrated that Modern
Greek can follow the common Polyglot small-seed pacing while asserting its
own structure very early through article/gender agreement, pro-drop,
Greek question punctuation, accusative articles, and especially θέλω + να
with dependent aspectual forms.
