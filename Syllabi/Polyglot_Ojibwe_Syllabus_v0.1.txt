POLYGLOT OJIBWE SYLLABUS

Version: 0.1
Status: Current
Encoding: UTF-8
Language: Ojibwe / Anishinaabemowin

1. PURPOSE AND GOVERNING PRINCIPLES

This syllabus defines the current canonical roadmap for the Polyglot
Ojibwe curriculum. It is governed by Polyglot Curriculum Charter v0.8.

It was written after a 100-card prototype rather than before card
generation. The prototype is therefore empirical evidence about useful
opening pace and structure, but it is not a transcript that future cards
must reproduce exactly.

The curriculum uses authentic contemporary Ojibwe from the beginning.
Double Vowel orthography is canonical. The University of Minnesota
Ojibwe People's Dictionary is the principal initial lexical/reference
anchor, supplemented by reliable grammatical, educational and
community-based sources.

Ojibwe is currently treated as documentation-limited for Polyglot
purposes. This is a statement about the limits of reliable accessible
documentation and generation confidence, not about the expressive
capacity or completeness of Ojibwe. The curriculum should proceed as
deeply and broadly as reliable evidence supports and leave uncertain
areas unresolved rather than manufacture forms, idiom, dialect
uniformity or natural usage.

2. VARIETY AND SOURCE POLICY

Ojibwe is not represented here as a single variation-free standard.

The initial prototype is anchored primarily in the Minnesota/Wisconsin
and related material represented by the Ojibwe People's Dictionary and
supporting grammatical resources. Documented regional forms may enter
the curriculum when useful and well supported.

Do not silently merge dialect forms into a fictitious pan-Ojibwe norm.
When a form is meaningfully regional and the distinction matters for
exact matching, disambiguate the EnglishPrompt or otherwise make the
intended variety clear. Ordinary widely supported forms need not carry a
regional label merely because variation exists elsewhere.

Dialect coverage should grow longitudinally as documentation permits.
Future card generation should prefer forms supported by the current
reference base while remaining open to Ontario and other Ojibwe
varieties as reliable sources are incorporated.

3. ORTHOGRAPHY AND INPUT

Use Double Vowel orthography from the first card.

Do not create a transliteration layer. Long and short vowels, apostrophe
/glottal-stop notation, consonant spelling and other orthographic
distinctions are part of the Target.

InputHint is normally blank. It may later be used only if a practical
input-method problem genuinely benefits from it.

Orthographic variation must not be normalized by guesswork. Where
reliable sources disagree, determine whether the difference is dialectal,
editorial or genuinely interchangeable before creating a canonical
Target.

4. EMPIRICAL OPENING FROM THE 100-CARD PROTOTYPE

The prototype established the following useful opening:

OJ-01 — Small noun seed

OBJECTIVE: Introduce a very small set of concrete nouns, including
animate and inanimate members, without beginning with grammatical labels.

CURRENT EVIDENCE: waakaa'igan, inini, ikwe, abinoojiinh and a small
number of additional useful nouns.

OJ-02 — Number as first evidence of animacy

OBJECTIVE: Establish singular/plural pairs slowly enough for the learner
to see that inanimate plurals characteristically end in n while animate
plurals characteristically end in g, without pretending that every noun
takes one mechanically identical surface suffix.

CARD STRATEGY: Use several controlled noun pairs. Include transparent
patterns before less-transparent stem/suffix combinations. Noun animacy
is lexical and must ultimately be learned with the noun.

OJ-03 — Inanimate and animate predicate verbs

OBJECTIVE: Establish that qualities English often expresses with
adjectives may be verbs in Ojibwe and that predicate choice can depend
on the animacy of the subject.

CURRENT EVIDENCE: agaasaa / agaashiinyi and
onizhishin / onizhishi.

CARD STRATEGY: English prompts should be genuine sentences when the
Ojibwe Target is a sentence: "It is small.", "He is small.", etc.
Where Ojibwe does not encode English he/she gender, use "he" as the
ordinary English example and occasional "she" where useful rather than
"he/she" or "s/he".

OJ-04 — Demonstratives and animacy

OBJECTIVE: Establish proximal and distal demonstratives across animate
and inanimate nouns and singular/plural number.

CARD STRATEGY: English already distinguishes this/these and that/those.
Parenthetical prompt labels therefore mark only information English does
not supply, e.g. "this (animate)" and "this (inanimate)", not redundant
"(singular)" or "(plural)" labels.

OJ-05 — Initial interrogatives and pronouns

OBJECTIVE: Introduce high-value interrogatives and independent personal
pronouns after the learner has enough known material to use them
meaningfully.

CURRENT EVIDENCE: awenen, awegonen; niin, giin, wiin.

OJ-06 — Initial VAI person morphology

OBJECTIVE: Let first-, second- and third-person contrasts begin to emerge
through natural animate intransitive predicates.

CURRENT EVIDENCE: noun-derived -wi predicates and mino-ayaa demonstrate
that an Ojibwe verb can constitute a complete sentence and that person
information is carried morphologically.

CARD STRATEGY: Do not preserve independent pronouns merely because the
English prompt contains a subject. Use them when contrast, focus,
disambiguation or the target construction calls for them.

OJ-07 — Dependent nouns and first possession

OBJECTIVE: Introduce possession through a small, well-documented set of
dependent nouns before attempting the full noun-possession system.

CURRENT EVIDENCE: =niijaanis- and =iinizis- families.

CARD STRATEGY: Treat dependent-noun morphology as a real structural
system, not merely vocabulary with prefixes. Expand person/number
coverage only where each new form teaches a useful contrast.

The first 100 cards introduce OJ-01 through OJ-07 unevenly. Introduction
does not imply establishment. Future cards should reinforce these systems
while advancing into the roadmap below.

5. EARLY STRUCTURAL ROADMAP

OJ-08 — Animate/inanimate system consolidation

OBJECTIVE: Make noun animacy reusable through plural, demonstrative and
predicate selection rather than through terminology cards.

Include semantically unexpected animate nouns where well documented so
the learner does not infer that animacy simply means biological life.

OJ-09 — VAI independent-order person and number

OBJECTIVE: Establish the major independent affirmative person/number
contrasts of animate intransitive verbs.

CARD STRATEGY: Use a small set of high-frequency verbs and let the
paradigm teach genuine person/number morphology. Do not mechanically
repeat complete paradigms for every new verb.

OJ-10 — VII development

OBJECTIVE: Expand inanimate intransitive predicates and their important
stem/inflection patterns.

NOTE: VII verbs have only third-person subjects. Do not force an
English-style personal conjugation paradigm onto them.

OJ-11 — Independent-order negation

OBJECTIVE: Establish ordinary negative clauses across already-known
intransitive patterns before adding avoidable transitive complexity.

OJ-12 — Polar and content questions

OBJECTIVE: Develop question formation beyond the initial ina, awenen and
awegonen examples using established vocabulary and morphology.

OJ-13 — Personal pronouns and inclusive/exclusive number

OBJECTIVE: Establish the useful independent-pronoun inventory, including
the Ojibwe inclusive/exclusive first-person plural distinction.

CARD STRATEGY: Pronoun cards may teach the pronouns explicitly; ordinary
sentences should continue to omit independent pronouns when natural.

OJ-14 — Noun possession

OBJECTIVE: Expand from dependent nouns into ordinary possessed animate
and inanimate nouns, including possession markers where required.

CARD STRATEGY: Develop possession through several noun types rather than
presenting one suffix as universal.

OJ-15 — Obviation: first controlled introduction

OBJECTIVE: Establish the proximate/obviative distinction for animate
third persons once ordinary animacy and possession are secure.

Begin with contexts where the grammatical relationship makes the
contrast especially recoverable, including possession where appropriate.

DELIBERATELY EXCLUDED AT INTRODUCTION: Treating obviation as an English
"fourth person" translation category. Teach its grammatical consequences
through Ojibwe examples.

OJ-16 — Obviative nouns and demonstratives

OBJECTIVE: Extend obviation into noun and demonstrative morphology and
make clear that animate obviative demonstratives do not reproduce the
ordinary singular/plural contrast in the same way as proximate forms.

OJ-17 — Locative nouns

OBJECTIVE: Establish productive locative morphology with useful place
vocabulary and natural clauses.

OJ-18 — Diminutives and other high-value noun derivation

OBJECTIVE: Introduce common noun-forming morphology where it is
productive and well documented, beginning with useful diminutives rather
than an exhaustive derivational inventory.

6. VERB-CLASS SPINE

Ojibwe verb classes are a central organizing fact of the language:

VAI — animate intransitive;
VII — inanimate intransitive;
VTA — transitive animate;
VTI — transitive inanimate.

The labels are useful for syllabus/generator reasoning. Cards normally
teach their consequences through language rather than asking learners to
name grammatical classes.

OJ-19 — VTI foundation

OBJECTIVE: Introduce transitive verbs with inanimate objects using
high-frequency, well-documented verbs and already-known inanimate nouns.

OJ-20 — VTI independent-order person/number

OBJECTIVE: Establish productive subject morphology without mechanically
exhausting every lexical subtype.

OJ-21 — VTA foundation

OBJECTIVE: Introduce transitive verbs with animate objects only after
animacy, VAI person morphology and initial obviation are sufficiently
secure.

OJ-22 — VTA local-person interactions

OBJECTIVE: Develop first/second-person subject-object relationships in
controlled contrasts.

OJ-23 — VTA third persons and direct/inverse morphology

OBJECTIVE: Establish the morphology required when animate participants
interact, integrating proximate/obviative structure rather than teaching
direct/inverse as an isolated terminology lesson.

CARD STRATEGY: This is a major paradigm area. Fill systematic contrasts
when each cell changes the participant relationship; do not fill tables
merely to substitute another verb stem.

OJ-24 — Transitive negation and questions

OBJECTIVE: Reuse established negative and interrogative structures with
VTA/VTI clauses.

OJ-25 — Four-class synthesis

OBJECTIVE: Make VAI/VII/VTA/VTI selection increasingly automatic through
contrasting natural clauses sharing related meanings where reliable word
families permit.

7. TENSE, MODE, PREVERBS AND VERBAL COMPLEXITY

OJ-26 — Past reference

OBJECTIVE: Establish common past marking with already-known verb classes.

OJ-27 — Future and prospective reference

OBJECTIVE: Establish common future/prospective constructions and their
interaction with the verbal complex.

OJ-28 — Lexical and grammatical preverbs

OBJECTIVE: Develop high-frequency preverbs in natural combinations,
including the orthographic conventions for writing them.

OJ-29 — Directional and relational preverbs

OBJECTIVE: Establish common direction, source, destination and relational
meanings as documented.

OJ-30 — Modal meanings

OBJECTIVE: Develop ability, necessity, intention, possibility and related
meanings through authentic Ojibwe constructions rather than one-to-one
English modal mapping.

OJ-31 — Imperative order

OBJECTIVE: Establish commands and requests after sufficient second-person
verbal morphology is familiar.

OJ-32 — Conjunct order: foundation

OBJECTIVE: Introduce conjunct forms in high-frequency subordinate,
interrogative and discourse environments where their function is clear.

OJ-33 — Independent versus conjunct contrasts

OBJECTIVE: Make order selection intelligible through controlled semantic
and syntactic contrasts rather than treating conjunct forms as merely a
second conjugation table.

OJ-34 — Initial change

OBJECTIVE: Establish initial-change patterns in the environments that
license or require them.

OJ-35 — Participles and clause modification

OBJECTIVE: Develop participial forms and noun-modifying/identifying
functions from established conjunct morphology.

OJ-36 — Verbal-order synthesis

OBJECTIVE: Integrate independent, conjunct and imperative orders across
known verb classes without demanding exhaustive simultaneous mastery.

8. FURTHER NOUN AND CLAUSE STRUCTURE

OJ-37 — Expanded possession and possessors

OBJECTIVE: Extend possessor person/number, possessed noun number and
obviation interactions.

OJ-38 — Numerals and quantity

OBJECTIVE: Establish common counting and quantity constructions with the
animacy and verb agreement they naturally require.

OJ-39 — Comparison, degree and quality

OBJECTIVE: Develop ordinary ways of expressing degree and comparison,
including lexical/preverb strategies, without forcing an English
adjective paradigm onto Ojibwe.

OJ-40 — Coordination and clause linking

OBJECTIVE: Establish high-frequency ways to join noun phrases, events
and propositions.

OJ-41 — Reason, condition and temporal relations

OBJECTIVE: Develop common complex-clause relationships using the verbal
orders and particles/preverbs already established.

OJ-42 — Reported speech, thought and quotation

OBJECTIVE: Establish ordinary reporting and quotation patterns from
well-supported examples.

OJ-43 — Reference tracking and discourse

OBJECTIVE: Deepen proximate/obviative choice, participant continuity and
reference across multi-clause discourse.

9. WORD FORMATION AND LEXICAL ARCHITECTURE

Ojibwe morphology makes word families especially important. Mature
vocabulary acquisition should increasingly show relationships among
initials, medials, finals, preverbs, prenouns, noun derivation and related
verb classes where those relationships are synchronically useful and
well documented.

OJ-44 — Verb finals and related class families

OBJECTIVE: Use transparent, documented word families to show how related
meanings recur across VAI/VII/VTA/VTI forms.

OJ-45 — Noun derivation

OBJECTIVE: Develop productive and high-value nominalization and other
noun-forming patterns through real vocabulary.

OJ-46 — Prenouns and noun modification

OBJECTIVE: Establish productive noun modification strategies without
importing an English adjective category where Ojibwe structure differs.

OJ-47 — Preverb families

OBJECTIVE: Expand lexical and grammatical preverbs longitudinally,
including semantic changes created by combination.

OJ-48 — Compounding and lexical families

OBJECTIVE: Use recurring morphology to accelerate vocabulary while
avoiding the assumption that every complex word is transparently
predictable.

10. DIALECT, REGISTER AND DOCUMENTATION

OJ-49 — Documented regional lexical variation

OBJECTIVE: Build recognition of important regional alternatives once the
reference form is secure.

OJ-50 — Documented regional grammatical variation

OBJECTIVE: Introduce regional inflectional or syntactic alternatives only
where reliable sources make their distribution sufficiently clear.

OJ-51 — Ontario and other regional strands

OBJECTIVE: Expand beyond the initial Minnesota/Wisconsin-heavy reference
base as reliable community, educational and linguistic sources permit.

OJ-52 — Conversational language and discourse particles

OBJECTIVE: Develop natural spoken interaction, discourse markers,
responses, hesitation, emphasis and stance from attested usage.

OJ-53 — Narrative and traditional discourse

OBJECTIVE: Develop structures and vocabulary encountered in documented
stories and narratives while respecting source, register and cultural
context.

OJ-54 — Contemporary institutional and public language

OBJECTIVE: Include modern educational, governmental, technological,
health, workplace and community vocabulary where established usage is
reliably documented.

SOURCE RULE: Never invent a regional label or assign a form to a
community from inference alone.

OJ-75 Lower-frequency concrete vocabulary.
OJ-76 Lower-frequency verbs and verb families.
OJ-77 Abstract and institutional vocabulary.
OJ-78 Idioms and fixed expressions.
OJ-79 Collocations and conventional phrasing.
OJ-80 Fine semantic distinctions and near-equivalents.
OJ-81 Formal, literary and ceremonial registers where appropriate and
reliably documented.
OJ-82 Historical/older language still encountered in contemporary
cultural or documentary contexts.
OJ-83 Regional comprehension across well-documented Ojibwe varieties.
OJ-84 Continuing lexical, grammatical and discourse expansion.

Unlike a well-resourced Polyglot language, OJ-84 does not assert in
advance that available documentation will support every educated-adult
domain to equal depth. Continue outward wherever reliable evidence is
strong. Where evidence becomes inadequate, record the frontier and stop
rather than filling the gap from analogy or model intuition.

13. CARD DESIGN RULES SPECIFIC TO OJIBWE

1. Animacy is learned primarily through consequences: plural morphology,
   demonstratives, predicates and verb selection. Do not clutter ordinary
   cards with "(animate)" or "(inanimate)" unless English otherwise leaves
   the intended Target ambiguous.

2. Demonstrative vocabulary cards may use parenthetical animate/inanimate
   labels because English this/that/these/those do not encode that
   distinction. Do not redundantly label singular/plural.

3. Where Ojibwe lacks an English he/she distinction, ordinary English
   prompts use "he" by default and occasional "she" where useful rather
   than "he/she" or "s/he".

4. Ojibwe verbs often translate complete English sentences. Capitalize
   and punctuate the EnglishPrompt as a sentence when that is what the
   Target means.

5. Do not invent an English-style adjective class. Quality concepts may
   be VAI/VII verbs, preverbs or other Ojibwe structures.

6. Citation forms must follow reliable Ojibwe reference practice for the
   relevant word class. Do not impose an infinitive-like verb citation
   strategy from European languages.

7. Exact matching makes dialect and orthographic ambiguity important.
   Disambiguate the prompt where necessary rather than accepting several
   unrelated regional Targets under one card.

8. Every generated form that is not directly attested should be licensed
   by a well-established productive paradigm from reliable sources.
   Exercise extra caution with VTA morphology, conjunct forms, obviation,
   derivation and dialect-sensitive forms.

9. Prefer attested example sentences when they fit the curriculum.
   Generated sentences are acceptable when their morphology and syntax
   are confidently supported and the combination is natural.

10. Do not make grammatical terminology the learner's task merely because
    generator reasoning uses labels such as VAI, VII, VTA, VTI,
    proximate, obviative, independent or conjunct.

14. DEVELOPMENT AND REVIEW POLICY

The current 100-card deck remains developmental. Pedagogical correction,
reordering or replacement may preserve or revise draft IDs according to
the Charter's development-versus-live-ID rule.

Before substantially enlarging the deck, audit the 100-card prototype
against this syllabus, especially:
- demonstrative regional forms and spelling;
- generated predicate+noun combinations;
- noun-derived -wi predicate forms;
- first-person VAI forms;
- dependent-noun possession forms;
- whether the current source base supports each Target as canonical
  enough for exact matching.

As confidence grows, generation batches may become larger. New
documentation or speaker/community evidence may require local correction
or a syllabus amendment.

15. COMPLETION AND REUSE RULES

1. Unit completion means sufficient canonical demonstration, not learner
   mastery.
2. Introduced material should recur longitudinally in later cards.
3. Vocabulary remains reusable once introduced.
4. Animacy, possession, obviation and verb-class distinctions should
   increasingly operate together rather than remain isolated lessons.
5. The scheduler supplies repetition; the curriculum supplies varied
   contexts.
6. Exact duplicate cards are prohibited.
7. Later cards may combine several established systems naturally.
8. Difficulty need not rise monotonically with card ID.
9. Regional breadth and documentation depth may grow unevenly.
10. The syllabus is a roadmap. Prerequisite-safe local detours do not
    require amendment.

16. AMENDMENT POLICY

Amend this syllabus when the preferred Ojibwe curriculum path changes,
when the reference-variety/source policy materially changes, or when new
evidence shows that a structural dependency or documentation frontier
has been represented incorrectly.

Routine card generation, ordinary local detours and expansion within
existing units do not require a new version.
